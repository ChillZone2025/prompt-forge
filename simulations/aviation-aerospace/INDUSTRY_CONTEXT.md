# Industry Context: Aviation & Aerospace

## What This Industry Does

Commercial and general aviation encompasses the operation, maintenance, and regulation of aircraft and the infrastructure that supports them. The industry spans airlines (Part 121 scheduled carriers and Part 135 on-demand/charter operators), maintenance repair and overhaul (MRO) organizations (Part 145 repair stations), flight training (Part 141/61), airport operations, air traffic management, and aerospace manufacturing. Practitioners range from A&P mechanics inspecting landing gear on a regional jet to dispatchers coordinating flight releases across a 200-aircraft fleet to safety managers investigating bird strike trends across a network.

The work is governed by an interlocking web of regulations — primarily the Federal Aviation Regulations (14 CFR) domestically and EASA regulations (EU) 2018/1139 internationally, with ICAO setting global standards through 19 Annexes. Aviation is a zero-tolerance industry for safety shortcuts. A missed inspection item, a miscalculated fuel load, or an ignored fatigue limit can kill people. Every task has a paper trail, every deviation requires documentation, and every return-to-service decision carries personal legal liability for the individual who signs it off.

The industry operates on a 24/7/365 cycle driven by flight schedules, weather, maintenance intervals, crew duty limitations (14 CFR Part 117), and regulatory compliance deadlines. Aircraft earn revenue only when flying — every hour on the ground for maintenance or operational delays costs the operator between $2,000 (regional turboprop) and $25,000+ (widebody) in lost revenue. This constant tension between operational pressure and safety compliance is the defining challenge of aviation work.

## Key Frameworks & Standards

### Federal Aviation Regulations (14 CFR)
- **Part 21** — Certification procedures for products and articles (type certificates, production certificates, STCs, PMAs, TSOs).
- **Part 25** — Airworthiness standards for transport category airplanes. The certification basis for all commercial jets.
- **Part 39** — Airworthiness Directives (ADs). Mandatory safety corrections. Non-compliance grounds an aircraft.
- **Part 43** — Maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alteration. Defines who can do what, documentation requirements, return-to-service standards.
- **Part 65** — Certification of airmen other than flight crew: A&P mechanics, Inspection Authorization (IA), dispatchers, repairmen.
- **Part 91** — General operating and flight rules. Baseline rules for all operations.
- **Part 117** — Flight and duty limitations and rest requirements for Part 121 flightcrew. Prescriptive fatigue rules: Flight Duty Period (FDP) limits, cumulative limits (60/168 hours in 7 days, 190 in 28 days), rest requirements (10-hour minimum, 30 consecutive hours in 7 days).
- **Part 119** — Certification of air carriers. Operating certificate requirements.
- **Part 121** — Operating requirements for domestic, flag, and supplemental operations. The rules commercial airlines operate under.
- **Part 135** — Operating requirements for commuter and on-demand operations. Charter, air ambulance, cargo feeders.
- **Part 145** — Repair stations. Certification, quality systems, personnel, housing, equipment requirements for MRO facilities.

### ICAO Standards
- **Annex 1** — Personnel licensing (equivalent of Part 61/65).
- **Annex 6** — Operation of aircraft (Part I: airlines, Part II: general aviation, Part III: helicopters).
- **Annex 8** — Airworthiness of aircraft.
- **Annex 13** — Aircraft accident and incident investigation.
- **Annex 19** — Safety management (SMS framework).
- **Doc 9859** — Safety Management Manual. The global reference for implementing SMS: safety policy, safety risk management, safety assurance, safety promotion.

### EASA Regulations
- **Part-M** — Continuing airworthiness management. Airworthiness review, maintenance programs, reliability programs.
- **Part-145** — Maintenance organisation approvals. European equivalent of FAA Part 145.
- **Part-CAMO** — Continuing Airworthiness Management Organisation. Replaced Part-M Subpart G.
- **Part-66** — Certifying staff (equivalent of A&P/IA).

### Industry Standards
- **AS9100 Rev D** — Aerospace quality management system (based on ISO 9001 with aerospace-specific additions). Required for most aerospace manufacturing and MRO.
- **AS9110** — QMS for aviation maintenance organizations.
- **MSG-3 (Maintenance Steering Group-3)** — Logic-based methodology for developing initial maintenance requirements for new aircraft. Basis for all modern maintenance programs.
- **AC 120-16 (Air Carrier Maintenance Programs)** — FAA guidance for developing and revising carrier maintenance programs.
- **AC 43.13-1B/2B** — Acceptable methods, techniques, and practices for aircraft inspection and repair. The mechanic's bible.
- **iSTARS** — International Standard for Training, Assessment and Reporting of Safety. IATA's safety data framework.
- **IOSA (IATA Operational Safety Audit)** — Standardized safety audit for airlines. Required for IATA membership. 900+ standards across 8 operational disciplines.

## Regulatory Landscape

### FAA (Federal Aviation Administration)
- **Airworthiness Directives (ADs)** — Mandatory corrective actions when unsafe conditions exist. Published in Federal Register. Compliance is non-negotiable — operating an aircraft not in compliance with applicable ADs is a violation of 14 CFR §91.403. ADs specify compliance time (e.g., "within 500 flight hours or 12 calendar months, whichever occurs first"), method (inspection, repair, replacement, modification), and documentation requirements.
- **Type Certificate Data Sheets (TCDS)** — Define the design approval basis for each aircraft model. Specify limitations, operating procedures, weight limits, and approved configurations. Any deviation from the TCDS requires an STC or field approval.
- **Supplemental Type Certificates (STCs)** — Major modifications to an existing type design. Avionics upgrades, engine conversions, interior reconfigurations. Each STC has its own Instructions for Continued Airworthiness (ICA).
- **Parts Manufacturer Approvals (PMAs)** — Parts manufactured under an approved design. PMA parts must meet or exceed OEM specifications. Controversial in some segments (engine manufacturers often discourage PMA for critical components).
- **Minimum Equipment Lists (MELs)** — Operator-specific list (based on the manufacturer's Master MEL) of equipment that may be inoperative for dispatch under specified conditions. Each MEL item has: a repair category (A = specific time, B = 3 days, C = 10 days, D = 120 days), conditions/limitations, and maintenance procedures (M items) or operations procedures (O items).

### EASA (European Union Aviation Safety Agency)
- Parallel regulatory authority for EU member states. Increasingly harmonized with FAA through bilateral agreements (BASA), but key differences remain in maintenance standards, crew fatigue rules, and certification processes.

### TSA (Transportation Security Administration)
- Security threat assessments for mechanics, crew, and airport workers. Airport badge requirements (SIDA access). Aircraft operator security programs. Cargo screening requirements.

### NTSB (National Transportation Safety Board)
- Independent accident investigation authority. Issues safety recommendations (not binding but politically powerful). Investigators have priority access to accident sites. Party system for investigations involving manufacturers, operators, and labor.

## Essential Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **AD (Airworthiness Directive)** | Mandatory FAA-issued corrective action for an unsafe condition in an aircraft, engine, propeller, or appliance |
| **MEL (Minimum Equipment List)** | List of equipment permitted to be inoperative for dispatch under specified conditions and time limits |
| **CDL (Configuration Deviation List)** | List of external parts (fairings, panels, covers) that may be missing for flight under specific conditions |
| **SB (Service Bulletin)** | Manufacturer-issued recommended (usually optional) maintenance action. Some SBs become mandatory via AD. |
| **STC (Supplemental Type Certificate)** | Design approval for a major modification to an existing type-certificated aircraft |
| **PMA (Parts Manufacturer Approval)** | FAA approval for manufacturing replacement parts for certificated aircraft |
| **A&P (Airframe & Powerplant)** | FAA mechanic certificate with airframe and powerplant ratings (14 CFR Part 65) |
| **IA (Inspection Authorization)** | Additional FAA authorization allowing an A&P to perform annual inspections and approve return to service after major repairs/alterations |
| **RII (Required Inspection Item)** | Maintenance task requiring dual inspection — one person performs, another (RII-qualified) inspects. Critical flight safety items. |
| **Return to Service (RTS)** | Formal signoff (FAA Form 8610-2 or logbook entry) that an aircraft or component meets airworthiness requirements after maintenance |
| **C of A (Certificate of Airworthiness)** | Document certifying an individual aircraft meets its type design and is in condition for safe operation |
| **ETOPS (Extended-range Twin-engine Operations)** | Authorization for twin-engine aircraft to fly routes beyond 60 minutes from a diversion airport. Requires specific maintenance program, dispatch procedures, and MEL restrictions. |
| **FDP (Flight Duty Period)** | Period beginning when a flightcrew member reports for duty and ending when the aircraft is parked after the last flight (Part 117) |
| **WOCL (Window of Circadian Low)** | 0200–0559 local time at the crew's acclimated location. FDP reductions apply when flights encroach on WOCL (Part 117). |
| **CAMP (Continuous Airworthiness Maintenance Program)** | FAA-approved maintenance program for Part 121/135 operators. Replaces prescriptive inspection intervals with reliability-based maintenance. |
| **NDT/NDI (Non-Destructive Testing/Inspection)** | Inspection methods that detect defects without damaging the part: ultrasonic, eddy current, magnetic particle, radiographic, dye penetrant, visual |
| **DER (Designated Engineering Representative)** | FAA-designated individual authorized to approve engineering data (repairs, modifications, STCs) on behalf of the FAA |
| **DAR (Designated Airworthiness Representative)** | FAA-designated individual authorized to issue airworthiness certificates, export certificates, and approve return to service |
| **8130-3 (FAA Form)** | Authorized Release Certificate / Airworthiness Approval Tag. Accompanies every part or component released from a Part 145 repair station. |
| **PIREP (Pilot Report)** | In-flight weather observation reported by pilots. Includes turbulence, icing, cloud tops/bases, visibility. |
| **NOTAM (Notice to Air Missions)** | Time-critical aeronautical information: runway closures, navaid outages, airspace restrictions, hazards |
| **SIGMET (Significant Meteorological Information)** | Weather advisory for severe conditions: thunderstorms, turbulence, icing, volcanic ash, dust storms |
| **Turnaround Time** | Time from aircraft arrival at gate to departure. Scheduled turnarounds range from 25 minutes (LCC domestic) to 4+ hours (widebody international). |
| **AOG (Aircraft on Ground)** | Aircraft unable to fly due to a maintenance issue. AOG parts orders get priority shipping — every hour costs the operator thousands. |
| **MRB (Maintenance Review Board)** | Industry/FAA body that establishes initial minimum maintenance requirements for new aircraft type designs using MSG-3 logic |

## Top 10 Common Mistakes

1. **Signing off maintenance without completing all steps of the task card** — Mechanics under time pressure skip or shortcut individual steps, then sign the entire task as complete. This is falsification of a maintenance record (14 CFR §43.12), which carries civil penalties up to $50,000 per violation and potential criminal prosecution. The ValuJet Flight 592 disaster (1996, 110 fatalities) traces partly to improperly documented maintenance on oxygen generators.

2. **Operating with AD non-compliance** — Failure to track and comply with Airworthiness Directives within the specified compliance time. AD compliance is not optional — it is a condition of the aircraft's airworthiness certificate. Operating an aircraft that is not in compliance with all applicable ADs violates §91.403 and §91.7 (civil aircraft airworthiness). Common failures: missing repetitive AD intervals, not accounting for amended ADs that supersede prior versions, failing to document method of compliance.

3. **Crew duty time violations (Part 117 / Part 135.267)** — Dispatching or operating flights that will cause flightcrew to exceed Flight Duty Period limits. Fatigue has been a contributing factor in dozens of accidents (Colgan Air 3407, 2009 — 50 fatalities, led to Part 117). Common mistakes: not accounting for WOCL reductions, ignoring cumulative limits (60/168, 190/672), treating rest facility credit as automatic when the rest facility doesn't meet the regulatory definition. The captain and first officer are personally responsible for not accepting an assignment that would exceed limits.

4. **Dispatching with inoperative equipment not covered by the MEL** — If equipment is inoperative and not listed in the MEL, the aircraft cannot be dispatched (period). Common mistake: assuming that because an item seems minor, it can be deferred. The MEL is a closed system — only items specifically listed can be inoperative. Using the MMEL instead of the operator's approved MEL is also a violation (MELs are operator-specific and must be approved by the POI).

5. **Improper parts documentation (missing or incorrect 8130-3)** — Every part installed on a type-certificated aircraft must have traceability documentation. For parts from Part 145 repair stations, this means FAA Form 8130-3. Common issues: expired shelf-life items installed, 8130-3 from non-FAA-approved sources, PMA parts installed where the MEL or AD specifies OEM-only, missing documentation for life-limited parts. Installing a part without proper documentation is a violation of §43.13 and creates an "unapproved part" condition — one of the FAA's top enforcement priorities.

6. **Fuel calculation errors** — Failure to carry required fuel reserves (Part 121: 10% of planned fuel, or fuel to the furthest alternate plus 45 minutes at normal cruise; Part 91: 45 minutes at normal cruise for day VFR, 30 minutes for IFR plus alternate fuel). Common mistakes: not updating fuel calculations after a route change, using incorrect performance data for the weight/altitude, not accounting for known delays (holding fuel), using taxi fuel estimates that don't reflect actual APU + taxi time.

7. **Weight and balance errors** — Incorrect center of gravity (CG) calculations. An out-of-CG-envelope aircraft may become uncontrollable. Common causes: using wrong passenger weights (FAA standard weights: 200 lbs summer, 205 lbs winter for adults, updated in AC 120-27E), not accounting for last-minute cargo changes, incorrect cargo loading (placing heavy items aft when CG is already near the aft limit), baggage bin assignment errors.

8. **Incomplete NOTAM review** — Missing a relevant NOTAM during flight planning. Runways closed, navaids out of service, temporary flight restrictions (TFRs), crane erections near the airport, unusual military activity. NOTAMs are published in a dense, hard-to-parse format — fatigue and volume contribute to missed items. Particularly dangerous: missing a NOTAM about an ILS out of service at the planned alternate airport.

9. **Deferring RII (Required Inspection Items) or skipping dual inspection** — Required Inspection Items exist because single-point maintenance failures on these items can cause catastrophic outcomes. Skipping the independent inspection defeats the purpose. Common pressure: night shift mechanics performing the work and signing both the "performed by" and "inspected by" blocks because no RII inspector is available. This is a serious regulatory violation and an immediate safety risk.

10. **Not reporting safety events through proper channels** — Pilots and mechanics who don't file ASAP reports, NASA reports (ASRS), or SDRs when they encounter anomalies deprive the system of safety data. Common reasons: fear of punitive action (despite protections), time pressure, belief the event was minor. The Swiss cheese model depends on reporting — every unreported near-miss is a hole in a barrier that won't be identified until it aligns with other failures.

## Excellent vs. Dangerous Work

### What Excellent Aviation Work Looks Like
- Maintenance signoffs that include complete task card documentation, correct part numbers with 8130-3 references, torque values recorded, and inspection stamps where required
- Dispatch releases that account for all MEL items, include correct performance calculations for the actual conditions (weight, temperature, runway contamination, obstacles), verify NOTAM review is current, and confirm crew duty time compliance with margin
- Safety reports filed proactively — even for minor events — with enough detail for trend analysis and follow-up
- AD compliance tracking that maintains a complete matrix with compliance method, date, next due, and cross-references to work order documentation
- Crew scheduling that builds in buffer for circadian disruption, monitors cumulative fatigue exposure, and doesn't treat Part 117 limits as targets to hit
- Ground operations that brief teams on aircraft-specific hazards, monitor FOD control, verify cargo is loaded per the load plan, and communicate delays and changes through the chain

### What Dangerous Aviation Work Looks Like
- "Pencil-whipping" — signing off inspections or task cards without actually performing or verifying the work. This is falsification (§43.12) and the root cause of preventable accidents.
- Dispatching flights with known MEL items beyond their repair category time limit ("we'll fix it at the next base") — this removes the aircraft from MEL authorization and makes the flight non-airworthy
- Pressuring mechanics to return aircraft to service faster by cutting corners on inspections or accepting "good enough" when the maintenance manual specifies exact procedures
- Operating in the WOCL without acknowledging the FDP reduction — "the crew said they feel fine" is not a regulatory basis for exceeding limits
- Using unapproved data for repairs — applying structural repair techniques from AC 43.13 when the aircraft's Structural Repair Manual (SRM) has specific instructions for that area. AC 43.13 is acceptable methods, not a substitute for manufacturer data when manufacturer data exists.
- Ignoring trend monitoring data — oil consumption increasing, engine vibration trending up, repeat discrepancies on the same component — and not initiating a reliability investigation until something fails

## Professional Certifications

| Certification | Issuing Body | Scope |
|--------------|-------------|-------|
| **A&P Certificate (Airframe & Powerplant)** | FAA (14 CFR Part 65) | Required to perform and sign off most maintenance on US-registered aircraft. Written, oral, and practical exams. No expiration but requires currency (activity within preceding 24 months). |
| **Inspection Authorization (IA)** | FAA (14 CFR §65.91) | Authorizes A&P holders to perform annual inspections, approve return to service after major repairs/alterations, and sign Form 337s. Requires 3 years A&P experience, renewal every March 31 (activity-based or FAA refresher). |
| **ATP Certificate (Airline Transport Pilot)** | FAA (14 CFR Part 61) | Highest pilot certificate. Required for PIC in Part 121 operations. 1,500 hours total time (1,000 for military, restricted ATP options exist). Written and practical exams. |
| **Aircraft Dispatcher Certificate** | FAA (14 CFR Part 65 Subpart C) | Required for Part 121 dispatchers who share operational control with the PIC. Written and practical exams. Dispatchers are jointly responsible for flight safety. |
| **Designated Engineering Representative (DER)** | FAA Designation | Authorized to approve engineering data (repairs, STCs, modifications) on behalf of the FAA in specific disciplines (structures, systems, propulsion, flight test). |
| **NDT Certifications (Level I, II, III)** | ASNT (American Society for Nondestructive Testing) | Qualification for non-destructive inspection methods per NAS 410 / EN 4179 (aerospace NDT standard). Level III can develop procedures and train others. |
| **EASA Part-66 License (B1/B2)** | EASA National Aviation Authorities | European certifying staff license. B1 = mechanical, B2 = avionics. Type ratings required for each aircraft type. |
| **IATA Dangerous Goods (CAT 1-12)** | IATA | Dangerous goods acceptance, handling, and shipping certification. Required for personnel who accept, handle, or load DG shipments. Recurrent every 2 years. |
| **AS9100 Lead Auditor** | Registrars (BSI, SAI Global, etc.) | Qualified to conduct AS9100 quality management system audits for aerospace organizations. |
| **SMS Implementer / Manager** | ICAO / Training Providers | Qualification for implementing and managing Safety Management Systems per ICAO Doc 9859. Not regulatory but increasingly expected. |
