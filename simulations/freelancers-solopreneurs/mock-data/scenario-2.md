## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **"Unlimited revisions" is a scope bomb:** Deliverable #8 explicitly promises "revisions until all stakeholders are fully satisfied with every page," and Section 5 of the contract guarantees "unlimited revisions." Given that the client's CEO has "strong opinions" but won't attend review meetings, the previous designer was fired for "not getting the vision," and the client's design direction is "we'll know it when we see it," this is a recipe for an infinite revision loop. Maya's own project history shows her worst projects (Nexus Dashboard: 7 rounds, Nexus Mobile: 9 rounds) were destroyed by unlimited revisions. She should know better. The contract must specify revision limits (2-3 rounds per phase) with additional revisions billed at an hourly rate.

2. **Payment terms are 100% on completion — no deposit, no milestones:** The entire $28,000 is due "upon delivery of all final design files" with net-30 payment terms. Maya will work 8+ weeks and deliver all value before receiving any payment. If the client is unhappy (or claims to be unhappy to delay payment), Maya has zero leverage. Industry standard is 25-50% deposit before work begins, with milestone payments at key phases. A 3-payment structure ($9,333 deposit / $9,333 at wireframe approval / $9,334 at final delivery) would dramatically reduce Maya's financial exposure and ensure client commitment at each stage. Given Maya's existing $4,500 unpaid invoice from Meridian and the $35K Nexus project with $0 collected, her cash flow management is already critical.

3. **Timeline ignores client feedback cycles:** The 8-week timeline allocates 0 days for client feedback at any milestone. In reality, each review round requires the client to gather internal feedback (including from a CEO who provides feedback "when he has time"), consolidate comments, and deliver them. Based on Maya's experience with Bloom Health (2-3 week feedback gaps) and the fact that this client's CEO reviews asynchronously, each feedback cycle could add 5-10 business days. A realistic timeline for this project is 12-16 weeks, not 8. The client's April 15 spring campaign deadline makes the 8-week timeline even more dangerous — if it slips (which it almost certainly will), Maya will face pressure to rush or cut corners.

4. **No IP transfer terms in the contract:** The contract does not address intellectual property ownership. Under US copyright law (17 U.S.C. 101), work created by an independent contractor is NOT automatically owned by the client — Maya retains copyright unless there is a written assignment. The contract should specify: (a) when IP transfers (upon final payment is standard), (b) what happens to IP if the project is terminated early (client gets what's been paid for, Maya retains the rest), and (c) whether Maya can use the work in her portfolio. Without these terms, both parties are exposed — the client may not legally own the designs they paid for, and Maya could face disputes about usage rights. For a $28,000 project, this is a significant legal gap.

5. **No kill fee or cancellation protection:** Section 8 allows either party to terminate with 14 days written notice — but there is no provision for compensating Maya for work already completed. If GreenLeaf cancels after 6 weeks (with 75% of the work done) and the 100%-on-completion payment terms mean $0 has been paid, Maya loses 6 weeks of work with no compensation. A kill fee clause should specify: (a) all completed work is paid for based on the percentage of project completed (or a per-phase breakdown), (b) a kill fee of 10-25% of the remaining project value to compensate for lost opportunity cost and committed availability, and (c) that any deposits or milestone payments already made are non-refundable. Given that the previous designer was fired mid-project, this is an especially critical protection.

6. **Fixed-price vs. hours mismatch — unstated risk:** The proposal presents a fixed fee of $28,000 but Maya's internal planning is based on hourly estimates (the deliverable list and timeline imply roughly 200-240 hours of work at her $125/hr rate, which would be $25,000-30,000). The client heard "fixed price" and expects $28,000 regardless of scope changes or revision rounds. Maya is thinking in hours but selling in fixed price — and the "unlimited revisions" clause means her hours could balloon to 400+ (as happened on the Nexus Dashboard: 120 estimated, 287 actual). If the project takes 400 hours, her effective rate drops to $70/hr. The proposal should either be genuinely fixed-price with strict scope controls, or convert to a time-and-materials model with a cap. The current structure combines the worst of both: the client gets unlimited scope, and Maya bears all the risk. Additionally, "Shopify implementation support" (Deliverable #9) is undefined — is it 2 hours of Slack answers or 40 hours of hands-on dev support? Maya is a UX designer, not a developer, and vague deliverables like this are how scope creep starts.
