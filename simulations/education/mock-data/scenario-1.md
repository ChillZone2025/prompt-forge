## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **Graduation rate uses a different cohort definition than the institutional (IPEDS) standard.** The department reports a 58% six-year graduation rate for the Fall 2018 cohort, but this uses a custom internal definition that includes late-declaring majors and excludes early-switchers. The IPEDS-standard rate for the same cohort is 49%. The custom definition inflates the rate by 9 percentage points. This discrepancy would be flagged by HLC reviewers and ABET evaluators, both of whom expect IPEDS-consistent data. Using a non-standard cohort definition without prominent disclosure is misleading in accreditation reports and marketing materials.

2. **Student satisfaction survey has a 12% response rate (not statistically valid).** With only 79 responses out of 660 students, the survey has a 12.0% response rate. This is far below the 30% minimum threshold for actionable survey data. Non-response bias is severe — the students who respond are likely systematically different (more engaged, more dissatisfied, or both) from the 88% who did not respond. Presenting these results as representative of student satisfaction in an accreditation report or program review is methodologically unsound. The demographic breakdown (68% male respondents) should also be compared to department demographics to assess representativeness.

3. **Job placement data counts "seeking employment" graduates as "employed" if they have any freelance income.** The department reports a 95.5% "placement rate" by counting all freelance/self-employed graduates as "employed" regardless of income level or field relevance. One graduate classified as "freelance employed" earned $2,400 total from two Fiverr projects over six months — this is not meaningful employment. Additionally, the placement rate is calculated only from respondents (67/108), ignoring the 41 non-respondents entirely. If non-respondents are assumed to have outcomes similar to the general population (a standard statistical approach), the true placement rate is substantially lower. NACE (National Association of Colleges and Employers) standards require distinguishing between full-time, part-time, and freelance employment and reporting outcomes for the full graduating class.

4. **Faculty publication metrics double-count co-authored papers.** The department reports 240 total publications over 5 years across 14 tenure-line faculty (17.1 per FTE). However, the methodology counts each co-authored paper once per department co-author. With 34 publications co-authored by two or more department faculty, the unique publication count is approximately 206, not 240. The per-FTE rate should be calculated on unique publications (14.7, not 17.1). While individual faculty publication lists legitimately include co-authored papers, the department-level aggregate should not double-count. This inflates the department's research productivity by approximately 16.5% — significant in a program review where research output is compared against benchmarks.

5. **Budget shows revenue from a grant that expires next year as recurring.** The department's budget includes $180,000 from Dr. Patel's DoD contract as a revenue line, but this contract is in its final year with no renewal submitted. The $110,000 NSF CAREER award for Dr. Singh has only 2 years remaining. Both are included in 5-year budget projections as recurring annual revenue. This overstates the department's revenue by $290,000/year in out-years and creates a misleading picture of financial sustainability. In a resource allocation review, this inflated revenue projection could be used to justify denying the department additional resources it will actually need when the grants expire.

6. **Peer comparison uses aspirational peers, not actual peers.** The department (14 tenure-line faculty, M1 classification, not nationally ranked in CS) selected Georgia Tech (#5), UIUC (#5), Purdue (#18), Wisconsin (#11), and Maryland (#16) as peer institutions. These are all R1 research universities with CS departments 4-5x larger. Every comparative metric makes Lakewood State look deficient: $840K vs. $42M in research funding, 49% vs. 84% graduation rate, $73.5K vs. $105K starting salary. Appropriate peers would be other M1 institutions with similar CS program size, regional draw, and mission — schools like Bowling Green State, Cleveland State, Youngstown State, or Wright State. The aspirational peer set produces a comparison that is meaningless for program improvement and misleading for resource allocation. ABET evaluators would expect peers selected based on program characteristics, not reputation.
