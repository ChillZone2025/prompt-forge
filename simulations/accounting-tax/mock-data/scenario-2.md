## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

### Issue 1 — Unreasonably Low Officer Compensation
David Park (65% owner, President/CEO) receives $48,000 in W-2 wages while working 50+ hours/week managing a $2.8M revenue digital marketing firm. He took $180,000 in distributions. The IRS "reasonable compensation" standard considers the duties performed, the company's revenue and profitability, and what comparable businesses pay for similar roles. A CEO/president of a $2.8M digital services firm in a major metro would command $120,000-$180,000 in compensation. Paying $48K and taking $180K in distributions is a textbook strategy to avoid employment taxes (FICA: 15.3% up to the Social Security wage base, 2.9% above). This is the #1 S-Corp audit trigger. See David E. Watson v. United States (2012) — Tax Court held that $24K salary for a CPA at a firm earning $200K+ was unreasonable and recharacterized distributions as wages.

Lisa Chen ($62K for VP of Ops) and Kevin Park ($38K for CTO) are also likely below reasonable compensation for their roles and the company's revenue level, though David's case is the most egregious.

### Issue 2 — Business Meals Without Required Documentation
$22,000 in "business meals" entered as a single summary entry with no supporting documentation. IRC §274(d) requires contemporaneous records showing:
- Amount of each expense
- Date and location
- Business purpose
- Name and business relationship of each attendee

Without these records, the entire $22,000 deduction is disallowable on audit. Additionally, business meals are only 50% deductible (IRC §274(n)), and the return should verify this limitation was properly applied. The $8,400 in "client entertainment" (golf, sporting events) is **not deductible at all** post-TCJA (Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 eliminated the entertainment deduction entirely under IRC §274(a)(1)).

### Issue 3 — Incorrect California Apportionment Method
The draft return uses single-factor payroll apportionment for California. This is incorrect. California requires **market-based sourcing** for service businesses under Cal. Rev. & Tax Code §25136, as amended by AB 2039. For services, revenue is sourced to the state where the **benefit of the service is received** (i.e., the customer's location), not where the work is performed.

Using market-based sourcing:
- California revenue: $1,856,200 / $2,814,600 = 65.9%
- California apportioned income: $980,400 x 65.9% = $646,084
- California tax (8.84%): $57,114

vs. as filed:
- California payroll factor: 63.0%
- California apportioned income: $617,652
- California tax: $54,601

**Understatement: ~$2,513** — but the methodology error is a compliance red flag regardless of the dollar amount. The correct factor should be the single sales factor (market-based sourcing) per California's current rules, not payroll.

Additionally, California imposes a minimum franchise tax of $800 on S-Corps and an additional 1.5% tax on net income passed through to shareholders (R&TC §23802.5), which does not appear to have been calculated.

### Issue 4 — Vehicle Deductions Without Contemporaneous Mileage Logs
Both vehicles claim 95% business use, but neither shareholder maintains a contemporaneous mileage log as required by Temp. Reg. 1.274-5T. Key problems:
- 95% business use on a personal vehicle is extremely high and will be scrutinized on audit. The IRS views anything above 75% with skepticism unless the taxpayer has a separate personal vehicle.
- David Park's log was "reconstructed from calendar" at year-end — the IRS requires records be maintained **at or near the time** of each trip. Reconstructed logs are routinely rejected.
- Lisa Chen's mileage is "estimated based on typical week" — this does not meet the substantiation requirement at all.
- Combined vehicle deduction: $32,190 at risk of full disallowance.
- If both shareholders have daily commutes to the LA office, those miles are personal (commuting), not business. This would dramatically reduce the business-use percentage.

### Issue 5 — Home Office Deduction Questionable for David Park
David Park claims a $12,800 home office deduction for his Encino residence. However, the company also rents a commercial office in Los Angeles where David conducts client meetings and presumably works regularly. For the home office deduction to apply:
- The space must be used **regularly and exclusively** as the principal place of business, OR as a place to meet clients in the normal course of business (IRC §280A(c)(1)).
- If David's principal place of business is the LA office, the home office doesn't qualify unless it meets the administrative/management activities test — the home is the only fixed location where he conducts substantial administrative or management activities.
- Even if the deduction is technically valid, a home office deduction **cannot create a business loss** for the shareholder. If the allocation of income to David through the S-Corp structure means the home office would generate a loss, the excess deduction carries forward.
- Kevin Park's home office is less problematic since he works remotely with no separate commercial office space.
