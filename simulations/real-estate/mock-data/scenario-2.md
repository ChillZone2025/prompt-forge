## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **CAM reconciliation includes capital expenditure (parking lot resurfacing) that should be amortized, not passed through:** The 2025 CAM reconciliation includes $67,500 for parking lot resurfacing — this is a capital expenditure, not an operating expense. Under standard commercial lease practice and most NNN lease structures, capital expenditures must be amortized over their useful life (typically 10-15 years for parking lot resurfacing) rather than passed through as a single-year operating expense. The lease's NNN provisions (Section 5.03) reference "Building Operating Expenses" but do not explicitly define whether capital expenditures are included or excluded. This ambiguity works against the tenant. If the $67,500 is passed through as a single-year expense, the tenant's 8.42% share is $5,683.50 — a massive unexpected charge. If properly amortized over 12 years, the annual impact would be approximately $473.96 to the tenant. The landlord's property manager describes it as "necessary" for "safety concerns," which is the standard justification used to characterize capital projects as operating expenses. A competent lease reviewer should flag this as a capital expenditure that must be amortized and recommend negotiating a CAM cap or explicit capital expenditure exclusion in the lease.

2. **Rent escalation clause specifies "Consumer Price Index" but doesn't identify which CPI index:** The escalation clause (Section 4.02) references "the Consumer Price Index published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics" but does not specify which index: CPI-U (All Urban Consumers — the most commonly used), CPI-W (Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers), or regional CPI (e.g., CPI-U Cleveland-Akron). These indices can diverge significantly — in 2022-2023, CPI-U ranged from 3.0% to 9.1% depending on the period, and regional indices varied by 1-2% from the national figure. The clause also does not specify the base period (month or annual average) or which geographic area's CPI applies. This ambiguity gives the landlord discretion to select whichever index produces the highest increase in any given year. A properly drafted escalation clause would specify: "CPI-U, All Items, for the Cleveland-Akron-Lorain, OH metropolitan area, for the twelve-month period ending [specific month] of each year." Without this specificity, the clause is an ongoing source of potential disputes.

3. **Personal guarantee extends 24 months beyond the lease term (unusual and risky):** Section 22.01 includes a "Guarantee Tail Period" of 24 months following the expiration or termination of the lease. This means Maria Santos is personally liable for lease obligations for 2 full years after the lease ends. This is unusual and significantly disadvantageous. Standard personal guarantees either (a) terminate at lease expiration, (b) "burn off" over time (e.g., guarantee covers Years 1-3 only, then terminates if tenant has clean payment history), or (c) are capped at a specific dollar amount (e.g., 12 months' rent). This guarantee has no cap ("shall not be capped or limited in any manner") and extends beyond the lease term. The tail period could be triggered if, for example, the landlord discovers environmental contamination after Rosewood Café vacates, or if there is a dispute over restoration obligations. Maria Santos' personal assets — house, savings, personal accounts — are exposed for 9 years (7-year lease + 2-year tail), with no limitation on the amount. A competent advisor would negotiate: (a) burn-off after 2-3 years of clean payment history, (b) a liability cap (typically 12-24 months' rent), and (c) elimination of the tail period or reduction to 6 months.

4. **TI allowance has a 90-day "use it or lose it" window that is nearly impossible to meet:** The TI Allowance of $144,000 must be requested within 90 days of the Commencement Date (June 1, 2026), making the TI Deadline August 29, 2026. However, the construction timeline shows: plans not yet approved by landlord (estimated April 10), building permit not yet submitted, construction estimated to start May 1 at earliest, and the contractor estimates 14-16 weeks to complete. Even if everything goes perfectly — permit issued in 2 weeks (mid-April), construction starts May 1 — a 14-week build puts completion at August 7, and a 16-week build puts completion at August 21. The TI disbursement requires completion, paid invoices, AND lien waivers from all contractors. Gathering lien waivers alone typically takes 2-4 weeks after final payment. Any construction delay — supply chain issues, inspection failures, change orders, weather — and the tenant misses the August 29 deadline, forfeiting up to $144,000. This is a landlord-favorable clause that creates enormous financial risk for the tenant. A competent reviewer should recommend: (a) extending the TI Deadline to 180 days post-commencement, (b) allowing partial disbursements during construction (progress payments), (c) tying the TI Deadline to substantial completion rather than a fixed date, or (d) at minimum, tolling the deadline for any landlord-caused delays (like the plan approval period).

5. **Exclusivity clause carve-out for "Online Sales" effectively negates the protection:** Section 9.04 grants Rosewood Café exclusivity for bakery/café operations, but carve-out (c) exempts "any tenant whose sales of food and beverages occur primarily through online ordering, delivery platforms, or mobile applications." In 2026, virtually every new food service concept has significant online/delivery revenue — many ghost kitchens and delivery-first concepts generate 60-80% of revenue through online channels. This carve-out would allow the landlord to lease the vacant Suite 170 (3,600 sq ft — larger than Rosewood's space) to a delivery-focused bakery or café that competes directly with Rosewood, as long as the competitor's "primary" channel is online ordering. The word "primarily" is undefined — does it mean 51%? 60%? 80%? Even a competitor with a physical storefront could argue that their "primary" sales channel is online if they push customers to order through an app. This carve-out effectively guts the exclusivity protection. A competent reviewer should negotiate: (a) eliminating the online sales carve-out entirely, (b) defining "primarily" as 80%+ and requiring the landlord to provide revenue documentation, or (c) at minimum, adding a distance/proximity restriction for any food service tenant regardless of sales channel.

6. **Landlord's insurance requirement specifies $2M umbrella but tenant's current policy is only $1M:** Section 14.02 requires the tenant to maintain $2,000,000 in umbrella/excess liability coverage. The tenant's current insurance schedule shows only $1,000,000 in umbrella coverage. Insurance certificates must be delivered to the landlord 15 days before the Commencement Date (by May 17, 2026). If the tenant cannot increase coverage to $2M — or if the cost increase is significant (typical premium difference between $1M and $2M umbrella for a food service business: $800-$2,000/year) — this becomes a lease compliance issue on Day 1. More critically, the lease does not specify a cure period for insurance lapses. If the tenant's umbrella policy lapses or is non-renewed (common in food service due to claims history), the landlord could declare a default. A competent reviewer should: (a) immediately obtain a quote for $2M umbrella coverage to confirm availability and cost, (b) negotiate a 30-day cure period for insurance-related defaults, and (c) confirm the tenant's current insurer will write $2M umbrella for a café operation (some insurers cap food service umbrella limits at $1M).
