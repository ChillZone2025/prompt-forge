### LCD L35041 — Transthoracic Echocardiography (Revised October 1, 2025)

**Key Changes (effective October 1, 2025):**

The Medicare Administrative Contractor (MAC) revised LCD L35041 to **require additional clinical documentation** for routine/surveillance echocardiography. Specifically:

1. **Repeat echocardiography within 12 months** now requires documentation of a clinical change (new symptoms, new murmur, hemodynamic instability, or change in clinical status). Previously, annual surveillance echos were covered for established valve disease and heart failure patients without requiring documentation of clinical change.

2. **ICD-10 codes removed from covered list:** R00.0 (Tachycardia), R00.2 (Palpitations), R06.00 (Dyspnea, unspecified) when used as the sole diagnosis. These symptoms now require a supporting cardiac diagnosis (e.g., I50.x for heart failure, I35.x for valve disease) to be covered.

3. **New documentation requirement:** The ordering provider must include a clinical indication statement referencing the specific clinical question the echocardiogram is expected to answer.

**Practice Status:** The practice has **not updated its echocardiography ordering protocols** since the LCD revision took effect on October 1, 2025. The cardiology department continues to use the pre-revision ordering templates, which do not include the clinical indication statement and frequently use symptom-only ICD-10 codes that are no longer covered as sole diagnoses.

## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **Systematic upcoding pattern — Dr. Sarah Chen:** Dr. Chen bills 99214 for 85% of her established patient visits, compared to a national benchmark of 45% for internal medicine and a practice average of 53.3%. The coding audit confirms the pattern: 6 of 10 audited encounters were documented at straightforward MDM (supporting 99213) but billed at 99214. This is not a documentation deficiency — the notes clearly show straightforward decision-making. This is a coding pattern where 99214 is the default regardless of clinical complexity. At 1,124 E/M encounters per quarter with an estimated 40% overcoding rate, the annual exposure is approximately $60,000+ in overpayments subject to recoupment. More critically, this pattern is a **fraud risk**: the False Claims Act imposes treble damages plus $11,000+ per claim. If Medicare or a commercial payer audits Dr. Chen using statistical sampling, they will extrapolate the overcoding rate across the entire claims population. An OIG audit using this methodology could result in a demand letter in the six-figure range. The practice needs immediate corrective action: coding education for Dr. Chen, prospective audit of her encounters, and consideration of a voluntary self-disclosure to the OIG if the overcoding is confirmed across a larger sample.

2. **LCD change driving echocardiogram denials:** The 34.4% denial rate for CPT 93306 (echocardiogram) is directly attributable to LCD L35041, which was revised effective October 1, 2025 — exactly when Q4 began. The practice has not updated its ordering protocols to comply with the new LCD requirements (clinical indication statement, supporting cardiac diagnosis codes, documentation of clinical change for repeat studies). This is a process failure, not a payer problem. Every one of these 64 denials in Q4 was preventable. The fix is straightforward: update ordering templates, train cardiology staff on the new documentation requirements, and implement a pre-submission edit that checks for covered ICD-10 codes before 93306 claims are submitted. The denied claims should be reviewed for appeal eligibility — some may have sufficient documentation in the medical record even if the claim lacked it.

3. **Timely filing loss on CLM-9187 ($8,200):** Claim CLM-9187 (knee arthroscopy, $8,200) was initially denied for a correctable coding error (missing modifier RT). The corrected claim was not resubmitted for 97 days — well past UHC's 90-day timely filing limit. The $8,200 is permanently lost. This represents a workflow failure: denied claims with correctable errors must be prioritized and reworked within 7-14 days to preserve filing rights. A single surgical claim loss of $8,200 exceeds the margin on approximately 60 office visits. The practice needs a denial management workflow with escalation thresholds — any denied claim over $1,000 should be flagged for same-week rework, and timely filing deadlines should be tracked with automated alerts in the practice management system.

4. **Humana Medicare Advantage contract below Medicare benchmark:** The Humana MA contract pays 85% of Medicare rates for CPT 93306 and is estimated to underpay by 5-8% across the full fee schedule relative to Medicare FFS. The contract was executed in 2019 and has never been renegotiated. On $512,800 in quarterly charges, this represents approximately $25,640-$41,024 per quarter in below-benchmark reimbursement ($102,560-$164,096 annually). With a 10% payer mix, this is significant. The practice should: (a) conduct a full fee schedule comparison against current Medicare rates, (b) calculate the total annual underpayment, (c) approach Humana for contract renegotiation with data showing the gap, and (d) evaluate whether the volume justifies continued network participation at current rates. Many practices have successfully negotiated 5-10% rate increases by demonstrating below-market rates with supporting data.

5. **Modifier 25 overuse:** The coding audit reveals that Dr. Chen (and potentially other providers) is appending modifier 25 to every E/M visit billed alongside a procedure, regardless of whether the E/M documentation supports a significant, separately identifiable service. In ENC-4452 (palpitations with ECG), the E/M evaluation and the ECG were part of a single clinical decision process. In ENC-4466 (skin lesion with biopsy), the entire visit was about the lesion being biopsied. Modifier 25 is one of the most audited modifiers in healthcare — OIG and commercial payers routinely flag practices with modifier 25 append rates above 30% for targeted review. Inappropriate modifier 25 usage results in payment for an E/M that should be bundled with the procedure, constituting an overpayment subject to recoupment. The practice should audit modifier 25 usage across all providers and implement documentation standards that require the E/M note to clearly document a separately identifiable service when modifier 25 is used.

6. **Dr. Chen as statistical outlier — audit risk:** Beyond the individual encounter-level overcoding, Dr. Chen's overall E/M distribution is a statistical outlier that payer algorithms will flag automatically. Medicare's Comprehensive Error Rate Testing (CERT) program and the OIG's use of statistical sampling mean that a provider billing 99214 at 85% will appear on audit target lists. The practice should benchmark all providers against CMS specialty-specific utilization data (available in the Medicare Provider Utilization and Payment Data) and proactively address any provider whose coding distribution deviates significantly from specialty norms. This is not about punishing Dr. Chen — it may be a documentation issue (she does the work but doesn't document the complexity) or a coding issue (the coder defaults to 99214). Either way, it must be investigated and corrected before a payer initiates an external audit.
