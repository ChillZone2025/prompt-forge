## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

### Issue 1 — DSCR Below Policy Minimum
The property NOI of $217,264 against annual debt service of $206,400 produces a DSCR of only **1.05x**. Most bank CRE lending policies require a minimum 1.20x-1.25x DSCR. A 1.05x DSCR means the property is barely covering the debt — a single tenant departure (Westfield Realty Group on month-to-month, $42K annual) would push DSCR below 1.0x. The seller's operating statement also shows $0 reserves for replacement on a 28-year-old building — a realistic reserve of $0.50-$1.00/SF ($14K-$28K) would further reduce NOI and DSCR. Additionally, no vacancy/credit loss factor is applied — standard underwriting uses 5-10% on actual rent for general vacancy and collection risk even when the property is occupied.

### Issue 2 — Revenue Discrepancy Between P&L and Tax Return
The borrower's P&L shows $1,164,000 in gross revenue while the tax return (Form 1065) shows $980,000 — a $184,000 (18.8%) gap. This is a major red flag. Tax returns have penalties for material misstatement (IRC §6662 — 20% accuracy-related penalty). P&Ls prepared by the borrower have no such consequence. The credit analysis must use tax-return-verified income, not the borrower-prepared P&L. If the borrower is on cash basis and claims timing differences, request 3 years of returns to see if the "timing" resolves year over year. If it doesn't, the P&L may be overstated to support the loan request.

### Issue 3 — Guarantor Has Negative Global Cash Flow
Victor Dahl's personal tax return shows AGI of $161,200. After personal debt service ($112,800) and estimated living expenses ($72,000), his available cash flow is **negative ($23,600)**. This means the guarantor cannot support the loan from personal cash flow — the guarantee is only as good as his net worth (which is largely illiquid real estate). The global cash flow analysis must include ALL personal and entity obligations. A guarantor who can't service existing debt adds no real repayment capacity.

### Issue 4 — Environmental Risk from Adjacent Properties
The Phase I identifies an active gas station (200 ft west, 2 UST releases on record with "monitoring ongoing") and a closed dry cleaner (150 ft east, closed 2019). Gas stations and dry cleaners are classic environmental contaminants (petroleum hydrocarbons and perchloroethylene respectively). The Phase I says "no plume migration to subject property per available data" but did NOT perform a vapor intrusion assessment. Under CERCLA, contamination from adjacent properties can create environmental liability for the subject property owner. The bank should require a vapor intrusion screening at minimum, and consider a Phase II Environmental Site Assessment before closing. Lending on a property adjacent to two known contamination sources without further assessment creates potential environmental lien exposure for the bank.

### Issue 5 — Tenant Concentration Risk
HealthFirst Urgent Care (NNN, national chain) accounts for $97,920 of $336,720 in rental income — **29.1% of total revenue**. If HealthFirst doesn't renew in 2030, the property loses nearly a third of its income. Additionally, Westfield Realty Group ($42,000/year, 12.5% of income) is on a month-to-month tenancy — they can leave with 30 days notice. Combined, these two tenants represent 41.6% of income with either no long-term commitment (month-to-month) or concentration risk (single tenant = 29%). The credit analysis should include a stress scenario showing DSCR if either tenant departs.

### Issue 6 — Missing Flood Determination
The loan file states "broker indicated property is not in a flood zone" — but no Standard Flood Hazard Determination (SFHD) has been obtained from a certified provider. Under federal flood insurance regulations, the LENDER must obtain a flood determination from an authorized provider for every loan secured by improved real estate. The broker's verbal assertion is not acceptable documentation. If the property turns out to be in an SFHA, mandatory flood insurance is required before closing. Failure to obtain a proper flood determination violates Regulation H and carries civil money penalties of up to $2,000 per violation. Additionally, the appraisal should reference the flood zone — if it doesn't match the SFHD, that's an additional discrepancy.
