The operation is paying a premium for purchased hay ($125 vs. $50-60 estimated production cost) because no hay infrastructure (mowing, raking, baling equipment) exists on the operation.

## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **Calf mortality rate 8.2% vs industry benchmark 3-4% — no root cause analysis performed; vaccination protocol is 2 years out of date.** The operation lost 35 calves from birth to weaning (8.2% mortality) when the industry benchmark is 3-4%. Scours killed 14 calves — the scours vaccine was removed from the protocol in 2023 to save $4,500/year, and scours deaths escalated from 4 (2023) to 8 (2024) to 14 (2025). The correlation is obvious but has not been analyzed. BRD killed 12 calves — the modified-live respiratory vaccine being used (Cattle Master Gold) was discontinued in 2024 and the remaining stock from 2023 is likely degraded (vaccine efficacy declines with age and improper storage). The entire vaccination protocol has not been reviewed by the veterinarian since January 2024. At the 2025 weaning value of approximately $1,050/calf, the excess mortality (35 actual vs. ~17 at 4% benchmark = 18 excess deaths) represents approximately $18,900 in preventable loss — 4x the cost of the scours vaccine that was cut.

2. **Feed cost per head 25% above regional average — all hay is purchased when pasture improvement and on-farm hay production could dramatically reduce cost.** Total feed cost is $889/cow vs. the regional average of $712 — a $177/head premium. The operation purchases ALL 2,400 tons of hay at $125/ton delivered ($300,000). Regional operations typically produce 50-70% of their own hay at $50-60/ton production cost. Rolling Creek has a 400-acre unused hay meadow (brome/fescue) that could produce 800-1,000 tons/year. Producing 900 tons on-farm at $55/ton ($49,500) instead of purchasing at $125/ton ($112,500) would save approximately $63,000 annually. Equipment investment would be required but typically pays back in 2-3 years. The operation is currently losing $281,974/year — feed cost reduction is the most direct path to profitability.

3. **Bull BSE testing not performed in 2025 or scheduled for 2026 — 2024 preg check showed 87% conception rate (below 92% target); 2 "questionable" bulls were retained.** The herd's 87% conception rate is 5-8 points below the 92-95% target. Bull fertility is the most common single cause of low conception rates. The last BSE was performed in spring 2024 — two years will have passed by the 2026 breeding season. Two bulls were classified "questionable" in 2024 and retained anyway. At $75/bull x 22 bulls = $1,650 for BSE testing — vs. the cost of a single subfertile bull leaving 20-30 cows open ($21,000-$31,500 in lost calf revenue at $1,050/calf). The cost-benefit ratio is approximately 1:15 at minimum. Six bulls are 8+ years old — fertility decline with age is well-documented, making BSE testing even more critical.

4. **60 cows over 10 years old with 55% conception rate — cull candidates not identified; herd carrying unproductive animals.** The 10+ year age group (60 head) has a 55% conception rate vs. 95.5% for cows 1-5 years old. These 60 cows consumed the same feed and occupied the same pasture as productive cows but produced only 33 calves (vs. the expected 55-57 at herd average rates). The 27 open cows in this group cost approximately $889/head to maintain = $24,003 in feed cost for zero revenue production. Additionally, 25 open cows from 2024 were retained to "see if they breed back" rather than culled — a common but costly practice. Aggressive culling of the 60 head in the 10+ age group (replacing with 60 productive replacements from the existing heifer development program) would improve the herd conception rate from 87% to approximately 93% and free up pasture capacity that is currently overstocked. Cull cow value at $1,086/head x 60 = $65,160 in immediate revenue.

5. **Market projections assume spring sale timing but historical data shows calves are consistently lighter than auction averages — backgrounding and retained ownership not analyzed.** Rolling Creek calves weigh 485 lbs at November weaning — 53 lbs (10%) below the 538-lb regional average. Linda plans to sell in spring at projected 510 lbs, but this implies minimal winter gain. No backgrounding analysis has been performed despite the fact that the operation's lighter calves would benefit most from additional feeding (value of gain is typically highest on lighter-weight cattle). A conservative backgrounding analysis (200 lbs gain at $1.00/lb cost of gain, selling 685-lb feeders in May) suggests approximately $41/head additional margin on steers. Across 175 steers, that is approximately $7,175 in additional revenue — likely more when accounting for the improved price per cwt at heavier weights. Retained ownership through a custom feedlot could add $100-200/head but requires price risk management (hedging or forward contracting).

6. **Pasture stocking rate exceeds NRCS recommendation by approximately 50 head (9.4% over capacity) — overgrazing causing documented forage degradation.** Actual stocking is 580 AU across pastures with a recommended capacity of 530 AU. Two pastures are significantly overstocked: Home Pasture (25% over) and West Range (30% over). The August 2025 NRCS visit documented visible overgrazing symptoms: bare ground, desirable species being replaced by increaser/invader species, declining forage quality. If current stocking continues, pasture renovation will be required within 2-3 years at $150-250/acre. West Range alone (600 acres) would cost $90,000-$150,000 to renovate. Reducing the herd by 50 head through the recommended culling of the 10+ year age group would bring stocking into alignment with carrying capacity AND improve herd productivity simultaneously. The overgrazing problem is directly connected to the refusal to cull unproductive older cows.
