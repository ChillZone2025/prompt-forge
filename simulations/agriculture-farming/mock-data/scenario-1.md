## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **Corn-on-corn for 3rd consecutive year on Home North (320 acres) and 4th+ year on River Bottom (400 acres) — yield drag and nitrogen cost increase not modeled.** Home North has been corn since 2023 (corn in 2024, 2025, and now planned 2026 = 3rd year continuous corn). River Bottom has been continuous corn since at least 2022 (5 consecutive years). The yield history shows the drag clearly: Home North declining from 224 to 209 to 198 bu/acre; River Bottom declining from 201 to 192 to 178. Documented yield drag for continuous corn is 7-15% versus rotated corn, worsening after year 2. Additionally, continuous corn requires 30-50 lb/acre more nitrogen than rotated corn (the plan applies 200 lb N on River Bottom but doesn't account for the additional N requirement from residue immobilization). The projected income statement uses a 210 bu/acre average yield for ALL corn acres — this likely overstates yield on the 720 continuous corn acres by 20-35 bu/acre, overestimating revenue by approximately $70,000-120,000.

2. **Crop insurance coverage at 65% when break-even requires 75% — underinsured by approximately $218,000 in risk exposure.** Break-even cost for corn is $785/acre. The 65% RP guarantee is $643.31/acre, leaving a $141.69/acre gap. For an additional premium of approximately $10,880, coverage could be increased to 75% RP ($742.05/acre guarantee), reducing total uninsured exposure from $309,528 to $91,232. The cost-benefit is overwhelming: $10,880 in additional premium to reduce downside exposure by $218,296. Selecting 65% coverage on a $2.25M gross revenue operation with 38% debt-to-asset ratio is inadequate risk management.

3. **Fertilizer plan based on 2019 university recommendations — not updated for current soil test results showing excess phosphorus.** The soil tests from October 2025 show Home North at 68 ppm P and River Bottom at 72 ppm P — both "Very High" by University of Illinois standards. At this level, the recommendation is ZERO phosphorus application. The fertilizer plan applies 60-80 lb P2O5/acre on these fields. On 720 acres (Home North + River Bottom), this represents approximately $25,000-30,000 in wasted fertilizer cost AND creates environmental liability from phosphorus runoff into waterways. The plan was never updated when the 2025 soil tests came back.

4. **Single hybrid (DKC64-35RIB) selected for all 1,760 corn acres including 600 acres of sandy loam/loamy sand at Sand Hills — variety not matched to soil type.** DKC64-35RIB is a premium hybrid optimized for high-organic-matter, well-drained silt loam soils (CSR2 120+). Sand Hills has predominantly Dickinson sandy loam and Onarga sandy loam (CSR2 72) with very low organic matter (1.6%), low CEC (8.4), and excessive drainage. This hybrid has no performance data on sandy soils. A drought-tolerant, lower-population hybrid with proven sandy-soil performance would be appropriate. At 34,000 seeds/acre seeding rate on sandy soil (where 28,000-30,000 is typically optimal), the operation is also over-planting by approximately 4,000-6,000 seeds/acre, wasting $15-20/acre in seed cost on 600 acres ($9,000-12,000 total) while increasing drought stress risk.

5. **Equipment loan payments create a cash flow gap in April-May that exceeds the operating line of credit.** The cash flow projection shows cumulative cash position reaching -$422,000 in May, but the operating line of credit is only $400,000 — a $22,000 shortfall. Monthly equipment payments of $17,900 continue through planting season when operating expenses peak. No contingency plan is documented. In practice, this means the operation could be unable to pay bills during planting — the most critical 30-day window of the entire year. Options that should have been analyzed: requesting an operating line increase, deferring one or two equipment payments through the planting window, pre-selling grain for cash advance, or restructuring the equipment debt to reduce monthly payments.

6. **No cover crop planned on 800 FSA-enrolled HEL acres despite NRCS conservation compliance requirement — risk of losing ALL USDA program payments.** The NRCS conservation plan for the HEL acres (Home North, River Bottom, Ridge Top portions = 800 acres total) requires cover crops after corn harvest. The 2026 crop plan shows "None planned" for all fields. Dave's comment ("We'll deal with NRCS if they come out") reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the risk. Conservation compliance violations can result in loss of ALL USDA program eligibility — not just on the 800 HEL acres but on all 1,800 FSA base acres. This includes ARC-CO/PLC payments, crop insurance premium subsidies, EQIP eligibility, and CRP eligibility. The premium subsidy alone on a 2,400-acre operation at 65% RP is approximately $25,000-35,000. Combined with ARC-CO/PLC payments, the total USDA program benefits at risk could be $50,000-100,000+ per year. The $30-40/acre cost of cover crops on 800 acres ($24,000-32,000) is a fraction of the potential loss. The next NRCS compliance review is scheduled for Fall 2026 — the same season when the missing cover crops would be most obviously absent.
