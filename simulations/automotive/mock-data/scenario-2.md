## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **2021 Tesla Model 3 appraised using 6-month-old market data:** The appraiser used a KBB value retrieved in September 2025 ($32,800) instead of current March 2026 market data. vAuto shows current market retail at $28,200 — an 18% decline driven by Tesla's new vehicle price reductions and increased off-lease/fleet supply in the used market. At the proposed $31,500 acquisition + $1,200 reconditioning = $32,700 total invested, the dealership would be $4,500 underwater against current market retail. This is a fundamental appraisal error — all valuations must use current market data, not historical lookups. A competent agent should flag the stale data, cite the current market values, and recommend either declining the trade or adjusting the offer to $24,000-$25,000 (allowing for reconditioning and target gross).

2. **Ford F-150 reconditioning estimate omits transmission service from inspection notes:** The service advisor's test drive notes specifically identify a transmission shudder at 35-45 mph under light throttle — a known issue with the Ford 10R80 10-speed automatic in 2017-2020 F-150s. The service advisor recommends a minimum $380 transmission fluid flush and adaptive relearn, with a warning that if the shudder persists, transmission replacement runs $4,200-$5,800. The appraiser's reconditioning estimate of $1,850 covers cosmetic work and brakes but does not include any transmission-related work. This is either a failure to read the inspection notes or a deliberate omission. A competent agent should flag the disconnection between the inspection findings and the reconditioning estimate, recommend adding at minimum $380 for the flush (bringing recon to $2,230), and note the $4,200-$5,800 risk if the flush does not resolve the shudder. With potential total invested of $33,080-$34,150 against a $31,400 market retail, the risk profile changes dramatically.

3. **Chevrolet Equinox has a branded flood title not flagged by appraiser:** The Carfax report shows a branded title — flood damage from Louisiana (September 2024, likely Hurricane Francine). The appraiser's notes describe it as a "clean-looking Equinox" and make no reference to the title brand. This is the most dangerous error in the batch because: (a) selling a flood-titled vehicle without proper disclosure violates state consumer protection laws and exposes the dealership to fraud liability, (b) many mainstream lenders will not finance branded-title vehicles, limiting the buyer pool, (c) the vehicle's actual market value with a flood title is 30-50% below clean-title value — approximately $8,400-$11,200 retail vs. the proposed $17,500, (d) at the $12,200 acquisition offer, the dealership is overpaying even for a branded-title vehicle, (e) flood damage creates hidden risks (electrical corrosion, mold, computer module failure) that may surface after sale, generating warranty claims, legal liability, and CSI destruction. A competent agent must immediately flag this as a no-buy or require a complete title disclosure and pricing adjustment.

4. **Three vehicles (Camry, Tucson, CR-V) priced 8-12% above vAuto market — will age past 60 days:** Vehicles 5, 6, and 9 are all priced significantly above current vAuto market data ($2,100, $2,300, and $3,200 above market respectively). In the transparent online marketplace, consumers comparison-shop on price. These vehicles will not generate competitive leads from AutoTrader, Cars.com, or CarGurus, will sit on the lot past 60 days, will accumulate floor plan interest (approximately $35-$50/day each), and will eventually be marked down to a price where they will sell for less than if they had been priced competitively from day one. This pattern is consistent with the broader aged inventory problem identified in Scenario 1 — the used car manager's philosophy of "not chasing the market down" is systematically creating the 35-unit aged inventory problem.

5. **Mercedes GLE 350 reconditioning ROI is negative — should be wholesaled:** The $4,800 reconditioning estimate on a high-mileage luxury vehicle at a non-luxury dealership produces only $1,600 in projected front-end gross (and that assumes selling at $24,200, which is $2,800 above vAuto market of $21,400). Even with back-end F&I gross at the dealership's current used PVR of $900, total gross is $2,500 against $4,800 in reconditioning — a net loss of $2,300 on the reconditioning investment. The correct decision is to wholesale at MMR ($17,200) for a $600 acquisition loss rather than investing $4,800 to retail for a net negative outcome. High-mileage luxury vehicles at non-luxury stores are margin traps. A competent agent should identify the negative reconditioning ROI, recommend wholesaling, and explain the principle: if projected front-end gross does not exceed reconditioning cost by at least $500, wholesale the unit.

6. **Ram 1500 appraisal ignores incoming redesigned new model launch:** The completely redesigned 2025 Ram 1500 is launching at area dealerships in mid-April 2026 — approximately 3-4 weeks from the appraisal date. When a full redesign launches, used values for the previous generation typically drop 5-8% within 60 days. The appraiser priced this vehicle at $34,800 (essentially at current vAuto market of $33,800), but the market is about to shift downward. If the truck sits for more than 30 days (likely at this price), the dealership will face a market-adjusted value of $31,100-$32,100 with $30,150 total invested — margin compression or loss. A competent agent should flag the incoming model launch, recommend either reducing the acquisition offer to account for projected depreciation ($26,500-$27,500) or pricing aggressively at $32,500-$33,000 for a fast turn before the new model depresses used values. Ignoring competitive product launches is a common appraisal error that creates instant aged inventory.
