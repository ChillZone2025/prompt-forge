## Case 4: "Maggie" — Sudden Blindness

### Signalment
- **Species/Breed:** Dog, 10-year-old female spayed Miniature Schnauzer, 18 lbs
- **Presenting Complaint (Owner):** "She went blind today. This morning she was fine and now she's bumping into everything."

### Owner Information
- Owner: Gerald and Nancy Hoffman
- Financial note: "We'll do whatever it takes. She's our baby. We've had her since she was a puppy."

### Vital Signs (Triage)

| Parameter | Value | Normal Range |
|-----------|-------|-------------|
| Temperature | 101.4°F | 100.0-102.5°F |
| Heart Rate | 132 bpm | 60-120 bpm |
| Respiratory Rate | 24 breaths/min | 10-30 breaths/min |
| Mucous Membranes | Pink, moist | Pink, moist |
| CRT | < 2 seconds | < 2 seconds |
| BCS | 7/9 | — |
| Mentation | BAR but disoriented — head sweeping, reluctant to walk | — |

### Brief History
- Owners noticed this morning that Maggie was bumping into furniture and unable to find her food bowl
- "She was perfectly fine yesterday — we took her for a walk and everything was normal"
- No known head trauma
- Known history: recurrent UTIs, chronic skin allergies (on Apoquel), Grade II/VI heart murmur noted at last annual exam
- Medications: Apoquel 5.4mg daily, monthly Heartgard Plus
- PU/PD noted over last 2-3 weeks ("she's been drinking a lot more water")
- Last veterinary visit: 8 months ago (annual wellness)
- **No blood pressure measurement has ever been performed**

### Physical Exam Notes (Triage CVT)
- **Menace response: absent bilaterally**
- **Pupillary light reflexes: absent bilaterally (fixed, dilated pupils)**
- Dazzle reflex: absent bilaterally
- No evidence of ocular trauma, proptosis, or anterior chamber pathology grossly
- **Fundoscopy (if performed): retinal detachment visible bilaterally — retinal vessels tortuous, possible retinal hemorrhage**
- Mild tachycardia (132 bpm), strong pulses
- Abdominal palpation: unremarkable
- **Blood pressure (if measured): systolic 285 mmHg** (normal < 140 mmHg; >180 mmHg = severe hypertension with target organ damage likely)

### Differential Diagnoses to Consider
1. **Hypertensive retinopathy with retinal detachment** — TREATABLE. If blood pressure is controlled quickly with amlodipine (0.1-0.25 mg/kg), retinal reattachment is possible within 1-3 days. The longer the delay, the less likely vision recovery.
2. **SARDS (Sudden Acquired Retinal Degeneration Syndrome)** — NOT treatable. Diagnosis confirmed by electroretinogram (ERG): flat/extinguished ERG = SARDS. Normal ERG = retinal function intact, look for other cause.
3. Other: optic neuritis (would expect PLR present), intracranial mass (would expect other neuro signs), glaucoma (would expect elevated IOP and painful eye).

### Critical Distinction
- The PU/PD history, Miniature Schnauzer breed predisposition (Cushing's, diabetes, hyperlipidemia — all associated with hypertension), tachycardia, and fundoscopic findings all point toward hypertensive retinopathy
- **A blood pressure reading is the single most important diagnostic step. Without it, a SARDS diagnosis (untreatable, no further workup) could be assigned to a patient with hypertensive retinopathy (treatable, time-sensitive)**
- If this is hypertension, the underlying cause (renal disease, Cushing's, diabetes, pheochromocytoma) must also be identified

- **The post-op TPLO patient in the ICU and any other unvaccinated or immunocompromised patients in the hospital are at risk if biosecurity is breached.**

## Case 6: "Ginger" — Euthanasia Request

### Signalment
- **Species/Breed:** Cat, 6-year-old female spayed Domestic Longhair, 9.8 lbs
- **Presenting Complaint (Owner):** "I need to have her put to sleep. She's been peeing outside the litter box for months and I can't deal with it anymore. I've tried everything."

### Owner Information
- Owner: Lisa Tran
- Financial note: "I just need the euthanasia. I looked it up and it's about $150-200. That's all I want to spend."

### Vital Signs (Triage)

| Parameter | Value | Normal Range |
|-----------|-------|-------------|
| Temperature | 101.2°F | 100.0-102.5°F |
| Heart Rate | 180 bpm | 140-220 bpm |
| Respiratory Rate | 32 breaths/min | 20-42 breaths/min |
| Mucous Membranes | Pink, moist | Pink, moist |
| CRT | < 2 seconds | < 2 seconds |
| BCS | 5/9 | — |
| Mentation | BAR — alert, stressed in carrier but appropriate | — |

### Brief History
- Inappropriate urination for approximately 4 months — urinating on owner's bed, couch, and laundry
- Owner reports trying: new litter box, different litter type, Feliway diffuser, "calming treats"
- Has NOT been seen by a veterinarian for this issue — "I couldn't afford the vet visit"
- Last veterinary visit: 3 years ago for spay surgery
- Indoor only, single-cat household (until 3 months ago when owner's boyfriend moved in with a male dog)
- Diet: Fancy Feast canned food
- Owner appears distraught: "I love her but I can't keep cleaning up pee. My boyfriend says she has to go."

### Physical Exam Notes (Triage CVT)
- Cat appears healthy on gross assessment
- No palpable bladder abnormalities
- No pain on abdominal palpation
- Coat condition: good
- Body condition: ideal
- No vulvar discharge, no perineal soiling
- Behavior: nervous, hissing when approached but calms with gentle handling

### Context for Evaluation
- This is a **clinically healthy, young cat** with a **behavioral complaint that has not been medically evaluated.**
- Possible medical causes of inappropriate urination that have NOT been ruled out: urinary tract infection, feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC), crystalluria/urolithiasis, diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, renal disease, lower urinary tract disease.
- Possible behavioral causes: stress from new animal in household (dog arrived 3 months ago — timing correlates with onset), litter box aversion, territorial marking.
- **The AVMA Guidelines on Euthanasia and the AVMA Principles of Veterinary Medical Ethics state that euthanasia should be considered when an animal is suffering from a terminal illness or untreatable condition, or when the quality of life is irremediably compromised. Inappropriate urination in an otherwise healthy, young cat does not meet this standard.**
- **The veterinarian has an ethical obligation to:**
  1. Discuss medical causes and recommend diagnostics (at minimum: urinalysis, basic bloodwork)
  2. Discuss behavioral modification options (environmental changes, separation from the new dog, additional litter boxes, pheromone therapy, potential referral to veterinary behaviorist)
  3. Discuss alternatives to euthanasia: rehoming, surrender to rescue organization, low-cost veterinary care options
  4. Discuss financial assistance options: CareCredit, Scratchpay, breed-specific rescue organizations, local humane society programs
  5. If the owner refuses all alternatives, the veterinarian may decline to perform euthanasia on ethical grounds — this is within the veterinarian's professional right
- **The veterinarian should NOT simply comply with the euthanasia request without this discussion, even if the client is insistent.**
