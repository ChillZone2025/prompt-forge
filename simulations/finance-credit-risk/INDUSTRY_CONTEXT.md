# Industry Context: Finance — Credit Risk

## What This Industry Does

Credit risk underwriting is the discipline of evaluating whether a borrower can repay a loan. Commercial lenders — banks, credit unions, CDFIs, and non-bank lending platforms — analyze financial statements, tax returns, cash flow projections, collateral, guarantor strength, and industry conditions to determine creditworthiness. The output is a credit memorandum that includes a risk rating, recommended loan structure, covenant package, and pricing. Every commercial loan a bank holds on its balance sheet started with an underwriter's analysis. Bad underwriting is what causes bank failures.

The daily workflow of a credit risk underwriter involves spreading financial statements into standardized templates, calculating coverage ratios (DSCR, FCCR, leverage), analyzing collateral values at liquidation rather than replacement cost, reviewing personal financial statements of guarantors, running UCC and lien searches, pulling credit bureau reports, and comparing borrower projections against industry benchmarks. For a typical $350K commercial credit facility, the underwriter must reconcile self-reported financials against tax returns, verify that all existing obligations are disclosed, assess whether offered collateral is unencumbered and sufficient, and produce a credit memo that documents the full analysis for the credit committee.

Credit risk underwriting exists at every institution that extends credit: community banks, regional banks, money-center banks, credit unions, SBA lenders, equipment finance companies, and alternative lending platforms. The underwriter sits between the relationship manager (who wants to book the loan) and the credit committee (who approves or declines). This position creates inherent tension — the underwriter must maintain analytical objectivity despite pressure to approve deals that preserve banking relationships. The ability to say "no" with documented reasoning is the core professional skill.

## Key Tools & Platforms

- **Moody's Analytics RiskCalc** — Produces Expected Default Frequency (EDF) scores for private firms based on financial statement inputs. The standard credit risk model for middle-market and small business lending. Maps financial ratios to probability of default using a proprietary database of defaults.
- **S&P Capital IQ** — Financial data, credit ratings, industry analysis, and comparable company financials. Used for industry benchmarking, peer analysis, and credit research. Provides credit default swap spreads and bond-implied credit risk metrics for public borrowers.
- **Bloomberg Terminal** — Real-time market data, credit analytics, and industry research. Used for monitoring public credit markets, rate benchmarking, and economic data that feeds stress test assumptions.
- **nCino (Bank Operating System)** — Cloud banking platform built on Salesforce. Manages the full commercial lending lifecycle: loan origination, credit analysis workflow, document management, portfolio monitoring, and regulatory reporting. The dominant platform for community and regional banks modernizing their lending operations.
- **Abrigo (formerly Sageworks)** — Credit analysis, loan pricing, and CECL compliance platform. Financial statement spreading, automated ratio calculation, stress testing, and portfolio risk analytics. Widely used by community banks and credit unions.
- **FICO LiquidCredit** — Credit risk decisioning platform for commercial lending. Automates credit scoring, risk-based pricing, and portfolio monitoring. Integrates borrower financial data with bureau scores and industry risk factors.
- **Dun & Bradstreet (D&B)** — Business credit reports, PAYDEX scores (commercial payment history), and company firmographic data. Used to verify business identity, check trade payment patterns, and assess overall business risk. D-U-N-S Number is the universal business identifier.
- **Experian Intelliscore Plus** — Commercial credit scoring model that predicts the likelihood of serious delinquency. Incorporates payment history, public records (liens, judgments, bankruptcies), and business demographics. Scores range from 1-100.
- **Equifax Business Credit Reports** — Commercial credit data including trade payment history, public filings (UCC liens, tax liens, judgments), and business identity verification. Used alongside D&B and Experian for a complete commercial credit picture.
- **CoreLogic** — Real estate data and analytics. Property valuations, flood zone determination, title/lien searches, and collateral risk assessment. Critical for any loan secured by real property.
- **CoStar Group** — Commercial real estate analytics. Property comparables, lease rate data, market vacancy rates, and cap rate benchmarks. Used when underwriting CRE-secured loans to validate appraised values and assess market conditions.
- **Trepp** — CMBS and commercial real estate loan data. Delinquency rates, loan performance analytics, and structured finance research. Used for benchmarking CRE loan performance and identifying market-level risk trends.
- **Baker Hill NextGen** — Loan origination and portfolio management platform for community banks. Workflow automation, financial spreading, risk rating, and regulatory reporting.
- **Fiserv (Prologue)** — Financial statement spreading and credit analysis. Automates ratio calculation, trend analysis, and peer comparison from borrower financials.

## Key Frameworks & Standards

- **Basel III/IV** — International banking capital adequacy framework. Risk-weighted asset calculations determine how much capital a bank must hold against its loan portfolio. Standardized approach assigns risk weights by asset class; Internal Ratings-Based (IRB) approach allows banks to use their own probability of default (PD), loss given default (LGD), and exposure at default (EAD) models. Basel IV (fully effective 2028) introduces an output floor limiting how much internal models can reduce capital requirements below the standardized approach.
- **CECL (ASC 326 — Current Expected Credit Losses)** — Requires banks and lenders to estimate expected credit losses over the full remaining life of a loan at origination, incorporating forward-looking macroeconomic forecasts. Replaced the incurred loss model where reserves were only booked when a loss was "probable." Methodologies include discounted cash flow, loss-rate, vintage analysis, and probability-of-default/loss-given-default models.
- **OCC Comptroller's Handbook — Commercial Lending** — The primary regulatory guidance for national bank examiners reviewing commercial loan portfolios. Covers underwriting standards, credit analysis requirements, risk rating systems, loan documentation, covenant monitoring, problem loan identification, and workout procedures.
- **Interagency Guidelines on CRE Concentrations (2006)** — Joint OCC/FDIC/Fed guidance establishing concentration thresholds: total CRE loans exceeding 300% of total risk-based capital, or construction/land development loans exceeding 100% of total risk-based capital, trigger enhanced regulatory scrutiny.
- **FFIEC (Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council)** — Interagency body that prescribes uniform examination procedures and reporting forms. The Uniform Bank Performance Report (UBPR) and Call Report are standard regulatory filings that credit departments must support.
- **Dual Risk Rating Systems** — Most banks use a two-dimensional risk rating system: (1) borrower risk rating based on probability of default (financial condition, industry, management quality) and (2) facility risk rating based on loss given default (collateral, guarantees, structural protections). Ratings typically map to a 1-10 or 1-12 scale aligned with regulatory classifications (Pass, Special Mention, Substandard, Doubtful, Loss).
- **PD / LGD / EAD** — The three core components of credit risk quantification. PD (Probability of Default) is the likelihood the borrower defaults within a given time horizon. LGD (Loss Given Default) is the percentage of exposure lost if default occurs. EAD (Exposure at Default) is the total exposure at the time of default, including undrawn commitments. Expected Loss = PD x LGD x EAD.
- **Stress Testing** — Projecting loan and portfolio performance under adverse economic scenarios (recession, interest rate shock, industry downturn). Required by Dodd-Frank for large banks (DFAST/CCAR) and expected as a sound practice by examiners at all banks. Applied at both the individual credit level and portfolio level.
- **Credit Memorandum Standards** — The credit memo is the permanent record of the underwriting analysis. Must contain: loan purpose, borrower background, financial analysis, cash flow projections, collateral analysis, guarantor assessment, industry review, risk rating justification, recommended terms and covenants, and risk mitigants.

## Regulatory Landscape

- **OCC (Office of the Comptroller of the Currency)** — Primary federal regulator of national banks and federal savings associations. Issues examination guidance, consent orders, and cease-and-desist orders. OCC examiners conduct targeted reviews of commercial loan portfolios, focusing on underwriting quality, risk rating accuracy, and concentration risk management.
- **FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation)** — Insures deposits and serves as primary federal regulator for state-chartered banks that are not Fed members. Conducts safety and soundness examinations. FDIC Risk Management Manual covers credit risk examination procedures.
- **Federal Reserve** — Primary federal regulator for state-chartered Fed member banks and bank holding companies. Sets capital requirements, conducts stress tests (DFAST/CCAR for large banks), and issues supervisory guidance on credit risk management.
- **State Banking Regulators** — Charter and examine state-chartered banks. Conduct joint examinations with the FDIC or Fed. State-specific lending limits (loans-to-one-borrower), usury laws, and licensing requirements for non-bank lenders.
- **Regulation B (ECOA)** — Prohibits discrimination in credit transactions based on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, public assistance status, or exercise of Consumer Credit Protection Act rights. Requires adverse action notices with specific reasons for denial. Applies to both consumer and commercial credit.
- **Fair Lending Laws** — Fair Housing Act, ECOA, and Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA). Regulators use statistical analysis to identify disparate treatment and disparate impact in lending decisions. Redlining, pricing discrimination, and steering are enforcement priorities.
- **Community Reinvestment Act (CRA)** — Requires banks to serve the credit needs of their entire assessment area, including low- and moderate-income communities. CRA ratings affect M&A and branch applications.
- **CRE Concentration Limits** — The 300% of total risk-based capital threshold for total CRE and 100% for construction/land development. Exceeding these triggers enhanced examiner scrutiny — banks must demonstrate risk management frameworks commensurate with the concentration.
- **HVCRE (High Volatility Commercial Real Estate)** — Acquisition, development, and construction loans that do not meet specific contributed capital requirements receive a 150% risk weight under Basel III (vs. 100% for standard CRE). Requires 15% contributed capital from the borrower to qualify for standard risk weighting.
- **Appraisal Requirements (FIRREA/USPAP)** — The Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act requires independent appraisals for federally related real estate transactions above $500,000 (commercial) or $400,000 (residential). Appraisals must comply with Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP). Evaluations are permitted below these thresholds but must still be performed by qualified, independent professionals.
- **Flood Insurance (NFIP)** — Banks must determine flood zone status for all real property collateral. Properties in Special Flood Hazard Areas require flood insurance. Failure to enforce flood insurance requirements results in civil money penalties.
- **Environmental Liability (CERCLA)** — Creates potential lender liability for contaminated property upon foreclosure. Phase I Environmental Site Assessments (ASTM E1527-21) are standard practice for CRE loans to establish the "innocent landowner" defense.
- **BSA/AML** — Know Your Customer (KYC), Customer Due Diligence (CDD), and beneficial ownership requirements. Commercial lenders must verify borrower identity, understand the nature of the business, and monitor for suspicious activity.

## Essential Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio)** | Net operating income divided by total debt service (principal + interest). Measures the borrower's ability to cover debt payments from operating cash flow. Minimum 1.25x is typical for most commercial loans. Below 1.0x means the borrower cannot service the debt from operations. |
| **LTV (Loan-to-Value Ratio)** | Loan amount divided by appraised value of collateral. Regulatory guidelines: 80% for CRE, 75% for raw land, 85% for improved residential. LTV alone is insufficient — a 65% LTV means nothing if the appraisal is inflated. |
| **Debt Yield** | Net operating income divided by loan amount. A collateral-based metric that measures the lender's return on the loan independent of interest rate or amortization. Minimum 8-10% is typical for CRE. |
| **Cap Rate (Capitalization Rate)** | NOI divided by property value. The rate of return on a real estate investment based on expected income. Used to value income-producing properties and benchmark market conditions. |
| **NOI (Net Operating Income)** | Gross revenue minus operating expenses, excluding debt service, capital expenditures, depreciation, and income taxes. The core measure of operating performance available for debt service. |
| **Global Cash Flow** | Aggregation of all cash flows from all entities and income sources controlled by the borrower and guarantors. Used to assess total repayment capacity across the entire borrowing relationship. |
| **Guarantor** | An individual or entity that pledges personal assets to guarantee repayment of the loan. Analysis requires a Personal Financial Statement, verification of stated values, and assessment of liquidity — not just net worth. |
| **Recourse vs. Non-Recourse** | Recourse: the lender can pursue the borrower's personal assets beyond the collateral. Non-recourse: recovery is limited to the collateral, with standard "bad boy" carve-outs for fraud, environmental liability, and voluntary bankruptcy. |
| **Subordination** | The priority ranking of a lender's claim on collateral. A subordinate lien holder is paid only after the senior lien holder is fully satisfied. |
| **UCC Filing** | A public filing under the Uniform Commercial Code that perfects a security interest in personal property. A UCC-1 financing statement establishes lien priority. A blanket lien covers all business assets. |
| **Blanket Lien** | A UCC filing covering all assets of the borrower. A prior blanket lien means any new lender's security interest is subordinate unless an intercreditor agreement is executed. |
| **Borrowing Base** | The maximum amount available under a revolving facility, calculated as a percentage of eligible collateral: typically 80-85% of eligible receivables plus 50% of eligible inventory. |
| **Advance Rate** | The percentage of collateral value that the lender will lend against. Varies by asset type: 80-85% for receivables, 50-60% for inventory, 75-80% for equipment at orderly liquidation value. |
| **FCCR (Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio)** | (EBITDA - Capex - Taxes - Distributions) / (Interest + Principal + Lease Payments). More conservative than DSCR because it deducts recurring non-discretionary cash outflows. |
| **Leverage Ratio** | Total funded debt divided by EBITDA. Measures indebtedness relative to cash generation. Maximum 3.0-4.0x is typical for most commercial borrowers. |
| **DTI (Debt-to-Income Ratio)** | Total annual debt obligations divided by gross annual income. Standard thresholds: 43% for qualified mortgages, 50% upper bound for most lending. In commercial lending, global DTI applies to guarantors and sole proprietors. |
| **Risk Rating** | The bank's internal creditworthiness assessment. Mapped to regulatory classifications: Pass, Special Mention, Substandard, Doubtful, Loss. Drives CECL reserve calculations and regulatory reporting. |
| **Covenant** | A loan agreement condition the borrower must maintain. Financial covenants: minimum DSCR, maximum leverage. Reporting covenants: timely financial statements, tax returns, compliance certificates. Breach triggers default provisions. |
| **Credit Memo** | The formal written analysis documenting the underwriting. Contains borrower background, financial analysis, collateral assessment, industry review, risk rating, recommended terms. The permanent record reviewed by examiners. |
| **Spreading** | Inputting a borrower's financial statements into a standardized template that normalizes presentation and calculates ratios automatically. Enables trend analysis and peer comparison. |
| **Concentration Risk** | Excessive exposure to a single borrower, industry, geography, or loan type. CRE concentration above 300% of capital triggers enhanced regulatory scrutiny. |
| **Workout** | Managing a troubled loan to minimize loss. Options: restructuring terms, requiring additional collateral, enforcing guarantees, or liquidating through foreclosure. |
| **Classified Assets** | Loans rated Special Mention, Substandard, Doubtful, or Loss. The ratio of classified assets to capital is a key measure of bank asset quality monitored by examiners. |

## Top 10 Common Mistakes

### 1. Ignoring Guarantor Liquidity
Underwriters accept guarantor net worth at face value without analyzing composition. A guarantor with $3M net worth concentrated entirely in illiquid real estate and equity in the borrowing entity provides virtually no independent repayment support. Liquidity — cash, marketable securities, unencumbered liquid assets — is what actually backstops a guarantee.

### 2. Relying on Stale or Related-Party Appraisals
Collateral valuations based on appraisals older than 12 months, performed by appraisers with a relationship to the borrower, or using replacement cost instead of market/income approach are unreliable. FIRREA and interagency guidelines require independent appraisals for transactions above regulatory thresholds.

### 3. Accepting Revenue Projections That Defy Industry Reality
Borrowers project 15-20% growth in industries growing at 3-5%. Underwriters who accept these projections without stress-testing against industry data and the borrower's own historical performance are building the analysis on fantasy.

### 4. Adding Back "Non-Recurring" Charges That Recur Every Year
Restructuring charges, litigation settlements, or owner discretionary expenses added back to EBITDA/NOI every year as "non-recurring" to inflate DSCR. If a charge appears in three consecutive years, it is by definition recurring. The loan is underwritten to a phantom coverage ratio.

### 5. Failing to Verify All Existing Obligations
Borrowers omit debts from loan applications. A UCC search, credit bureau pull, and direct verification against tax returns and bank statements are minimum requirements. An undisclosed $95K equipment loan at another bank changes total leverage, DTI, and the global cash flow picture entirely.

### 6. Ignoring Existing Liens on Offered Collateral
A borrower offers inventory as collateral, but a UCC search reveals a prior blanket lien. The offered collateral is already encumbered — the new lender would be subordinate with effectively zero recovery in liquidation. Accepting collateral without a lien search is underwriting malpractice.

### 7. Calculating Coverage Ratios with Inconsistent Income Sources
Mixing self-reported revenue with tax return income, or using projected income instead of historical. If the P&L shows $2.4M in revenue but the tax return shows $1.18M, the underwriter must use the lower verified figure or document a credible reconciliation.

### 8. Ignoring Concentration Risk at the Portfolio Level
Approving another CRE loan when the bank's CRE concentration already exceeds 300% of capital. Individual loan decisions must consider portfolio-level risk. Examiners evaluate concentration management as a core credit risk discipline.

### 9. Inadequate Covenant Structures
Setting covenants that only trigger after the borrower is already in deep distress. Covenants should provide early warning — typically set 15-25% below the approved level. A covenant that only triggers at the point of default provides no protective value.

### 10. Drafting Misleading Credit Memos Under Relationship Pressure
Writing a credit memo that emphasizes positives and minimizes known risk factors because a relationship manager is pushing for approval. The credit memo is a regulatory document. A memo that omits material risks is a potential safety-and-soundness, fair lending, and fraud issue.

## Excellent vs. Dangerous Work

### Excellent Work
- DSCR calculated on a fully adjusted basis with transparent addback methodology — every addback justified and limited to truly non-recurring items
- Collateral valued at orderly liquidation value with independent appraisals current within 12 months
- Cash flow projections stress-tested against downside scenarios: revenue decline, rate increase, customer concentration loss
- Revenue discrepancies between self-reported financials and tax returns identified and fully reconciled before any ratio is calculated
- UCC search completed, all existing liens identified, collateral coverage calculated net of prior liens
- Guarantor analysis deducts equity in the borrowing entity, discounts illiquid assets, and verifies stated values
- Covenant structure calibrated to provide early warning with realistic trip levels
- Credit memo documents all risks plainly, even when the recommendation is approval
- The underwriter says "no" when the analysis does not support approval, regardless of relationship pressure

### Dangerous Work
- Approving loans based on self-reported revenue that contradicts tax returns without reconciliation
- Calculating DSCR with recurring charges added back as "non-recurring"
- Accepting collateral without running a UCC/lien search
- Ignoring undisclosed debts that materially change the leverage picture
- Valuing collateral at replacement cost instead of liquidation value
- Accepting a related-party appraisal without flagging the independence violation
- Writing credit memos that bury or omit known risks to facilitate approval
- Approving a loan with DTI above 70% without restructuring or additional support
- Setting covenants so loose they provide no protective value
- Skipping environmental review on real property collateral

## Professional Certifications

| Certification | Full Name | Relevance |
|--------------|-----------|-----------|
| **CRC** | Credit Risk Certification (RMA) | The Risk Management Association's credential for credit risk professionals. Covers credit analysis, portfolio management, problem loan resolution, and regulatory compliance. The most directly relevant certification for commercial credit underwriters. |
| **FRM** | Financial Risk Manager (GARP) | Global Association of Risk Professionals credential covering market risk, credit risk, operational risk, and Basel framework. Quantitative focus on PD/LGD/EAD modeling and stress testing. |
| **CFA** | Chartered Financial Analyst | Three-level exam covering financial analysis, valuation, and portfolio management. Strong foundation in financial statement analysis and credit research. |
| **CRCM** | Certified Regulatory Compliance Manager (ABA) | American Bankers Association credential covering BSA/AML, fair lending, ECOA, CRA, and regulatory requirements that intersect with credit decisions. |
| **CCRA** | Certified Credit Research Analyst | Credential focused on credit research methodology, financial statement analysis, and credit rating frameworks. Relevant for credit analysts at banks and rating agencies. |
| **CPA** | Certified Public Accountant | Primarily an accounting credential, but CPA holders bring deep financial statement analysis skills critical for spreading financials and identifying accounting irregularities in borrower documents. |
| **CAMS** | Certified Anti-Money Laundering Specialist (ACAMS) | Covers BSA/AML compliance, KYC procedures, and suspicious activity monitoring. Relevant for credit professionals handling CDD and beneficial ownership verification. |
| **CFE** | Certified Fraud Examiner (ACFE) | Covers fraud prevention, detection, and investigation. Relevant for identifying borrower misrepresentation, inflated financials, and application fraud in the underwriting process. |
