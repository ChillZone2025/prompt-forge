## Planted Issues Summary

> **For evaluator reference only — not shown to the agent.**

### Issue 1: Social Security — Both Claiming at 62 (Leaving $185K-$245K on the Table)
David and Karen plan to both claim Social Security at 62. This is the default plan for most pre-retirees who haven't done the analysis. The optimized strategy: delay David's claim to 70 (benefit increases from $2,296 to $4,067/month — a 77% increase) and have Karen claim at 67 (full benefit). The delay costs ~$165K in foregone benefits (David's years 63-70 with no SS income) but gains ~$350K-$410K in additional lifetime benefits (assuming life expectancy of 85). The survivor benefit is the critical factor: if David dies first, Karen receives his higher benefit. Claiming at 62 locks in a low survivor benefit permanently. **The agent should run a break-even analysis and discuss the survivor benefit angle specifically.**

### Issue 2: Portfolio 80% Equities — Too Aggressive for 5 Years to Retirement
At 80% equities, the Lindströms are significantly over-allocated to stocks relative to every major target-date fund and age-based rule. A 2022-style bear market (-25% equities) would reduce their portfolio by ~$190K, potentially delaying retirement. They should be at 50-60% equities. The transition should be gradual (reduce equity allocation by 4-5% per year over the next 5 years) and tax-efficient (rebalance in tax-advantaged accounts first, use new contributions to buy bonds, avoid triggering gains in taxable accounts). **The individual tech stocks in David's Traditional IRA are a concentration risk within the equity allocation — 5 individual tech stocks is not diversification.**

### Issue 3: No Roth Conversion Ladder Planned
The Lindströms have $920K in Traditional/pre-tax accounts and only $34K in Roth. They'll retire at 63 and RMDs start at 73 — a 10-year window where their income drops from $291K to ~$167K. The 5 years between retirement (63) and Social Security (if delayed to 67-70) are especially powerful for Roth conversions because taxable income is lowest. They could convert $50K-$80K/year at the 12-22% bracket (filling up to the 22% or 24% bracket) rather than paying 22-24%+ when RMDs force distributions. Over 10 years, they could convert $500K-$800K to Roth, saving $50K-$120K in lifetime taxes. **Also critical: Karen's SEP-IRA balance creates a pro-rata problem for her backdoor Roth contributions. She should roll the SEP-IRA into her Solo 401(k) to clear the pro-rata issue before retirement.**

### Issue 4: Estate Planning Documents 12 Years Old (Pre-TCJA, Pre-SECURE Act)
The trust and estate documents were last updated in 2013. Since then: (1) The SECURE Act (2019) eliminated the stretch IRA for most non-spouse beneficiaries — inherited IRAs must be depleted within 10 years, potentially creating tax bombs for the children. The trust may have provisions referencing stretch distributions that no longer work. (2) TCJA (2017) doubled the estate tax exemption (now ~$13.6M per person) — trust provisions designed for a lower exemption may be unnecessarily restrictive. (3) Karen's SEP-IRA still lists David's mother (deceased 2020) as beneficiary — this creates an estate settlement nightmare. (4) The trust distributes to children "at age 30" — both children are now past 30, but there are no provisions for grandchildren, incapacity, or divorce protection. (5) Life insurance policies expire in 2027-2028, pre-retirement — they may need new coverage to bridge the gap or may no longer need the same amount.

### Issue 5: Healthcare Cost Estimate Doesn't Account for Medicare Gap (Ages 60-65)
The Lindströms estimate $12K/year for healthcare. Actual costs will be $24K-$36K/year during the pre-Medicare gap (ages 61-65 for Karen, ages 63-65 for David). Total underestimation: $60K-$90K over the gap years. COBRA only lasts 18 months. ACA marketplace premiums at their income level will be $1,200-$1,500/person/month with minimal subsidies. This alone could force an extra $60K-$90K in portfolio withdrawals during the critical early retirement years. **The agent should recommend modeling healthcare costs explicitly in the retirement income plan and considering ACA subsidy optimization (managing MAGI to stay below cliff thresholds).**

### Issue 6: Pension Lump Sum vs. Annuity Not Analyzed
David plans to take the monthly pension by default. But the lump sum option ($780K) deserves analysis: (1) The lump sum can be rolled into an IRA and invested — at a 5% withdrawal rate, it generates $39K/year vs. the annuity's $62.8K. The annuity wins on raw income. (2) However: the pension has NO COLA — $62.8K in year 1 is worth ~$38K in purchasing power after 20 years (at 2.5% inflation). (3) The lump sum provides flexibility: Roth conversions, legacy planning, control over withdrawal timing. (4) The pension's 50% survivor benefit ($31.4K) vs. the full IRA inheritance for Karen. (5) At current interest rates, the lump sum may be relatively less attractive (higher rates = lower lump sum). **The agent should walk through the comparison using a discount rate analysis and factor in: inflation, mortality risk (what if David dies at 70?), survivor needs, and the rest of the financial picture (they have other income sources, which reduces the need for guaranteed pension income).**
