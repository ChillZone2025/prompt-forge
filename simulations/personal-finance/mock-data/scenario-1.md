## Planted Issues Summary

> **For evaluator reference only — not shown to the agent.**

### Issue 1: Traditional 401(k) Instead of Roth 401(k)
Priya contributes 100% pre-tax to her Traditional 401(k). At 32, she's currently in the 32% federal bracket but has significant room to grow in her career (senior → staff → principal engineer, or management track). Her income is likely to be higher in retirement when RMDs, Social Security, and investment income stack up — especially given her high savings rate. The employer plan offers a Roth 401(k) option. At minimum, she should split contributions, and a strong argument exists for 100% Roth given her age and trajectory. **Counterargument that makes this nuanced:** She's currently at 32% marginal, which is relatively high — the Roth decision is less obvious than for someone at 22-24%. The agent should acknowledge this is a judgment call rather than a slam-dunk.

### Issue 2: Wrong Debt Payoff Priority Order
Priya is paying $135/month extra toward her 4.5% student loans while carrying $8,100 on a 21.99% APR credit card. The mathematically correct order (debt avalanche) is to pay the credit card first. The $200 extra she pays on the credit card should be $335/month (redirect the student loan extra payments), which would eliminate the credit card debt in ~14 months instead of ~20. Interest saved: approximately $1,200-$1,500 over the payoff period.

### Issue 3: Concentrated Stock Position (MRDN)
MRDN stock represents 31.1% of net worth and 25.7% of investable assets. Priya has never sold a share beyond sell-to-cover for taxes. This is a classic tech employee trap: her income AND a third of her wealth are tied to the same company. If MRDN has a bad year (layoffs + stock decline), she loses on both fronts. She should establish a systematic selling plan (e.g., sell 25-50% of vested shares each quarter and diversify into broad index funds). Capital gains exist but are manageable (~$14,400 unrealized, taxed at 15% LTCG rate = ~$2,160 in federal tax).

### Issue 4: HSA-Eligible But Not Contributing
Priya is enrolled in an HDHP but has no HSA. She's missing the triple tax advantage: pre-tax contribution ($4,150 individual in 2025), tax-free growth, and tax-free qualified medical withdrawals. At her 32% federal + 9.3% CA state marginal rates, the immediate tax savings from a max HSA contribution is ~$1,714/year. Over 30 years invested in index funds (7% real return), that $4,150/year grows to ~$415K in today's dollars — all tax-free for medical expenses (which will be abundant in retirement). This is arguably the single biggest optimization available to her.

### Issue 5: Emergency Fund Earning 0.01% Instead of 4.5%+
$31,600 in Chase checking/savings at 0.01% APY. Moving $28,000 to a HYSA (Marcus, Ally, Wealthfront — all 4.0-4.5% APY) while keeping $3,600 in checking for monthly cash flow would earn ~$1,260/year in risk-free interest. Same FDIC protection, same liquidity (1-2 day transfer). This is free money — zero effort, zero risk, zero downside.

### Issue 6: Overlapping Insurance Coverage ($340/month)
Priya pays $180/month ($2,160/year) for a $1M personal umbrella policy — expensive for a single renter with a Honda Civic. A typical $1M umbrella for her profile should be $15-$25/month. Either the policy is wildly overpriced, or there's a misunderstanding about what it covers. The $45/month Asurion electronics plan overlaps significantly with both her renter's insurance ($50K personal property coverage) and credit card purchase/extended warranty protection (Chase Sapphire Reserve covers electronics if she has that card tier). She's likely triple-paying for electronics coverage. Combined potential savings: $200-$260/month by right-sizing the umbrella and dropping the redundant electronics plan.
