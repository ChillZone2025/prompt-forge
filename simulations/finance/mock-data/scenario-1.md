## Planted Issues — Answer Key

> These issues are deliberately embedded in the data above for simulation testing. The agent being tested should identify them during analysis.

### Issue 1: Intercompany Balance Mismatch — UK Sub ($47,000)

**Location:** Intercompany Transaction Log — UK Sub section

**The Problem:** The US Parent records intercompany receivable from UK Sub at $1,247,000, but the UK Sub records intercompany payable at $1,200,000 — a $47,000 mismatch that will not eliminate cleanly in consolidation.

**Root Cause:** The US Parent recorded the Q4 intercompany charges at the monthly transaction rates (1.2600, 1.2500, 1.2800), accumulating $917,180 of new charges. The UK Sub, however, translated its full payable balance at the December 31 spot rate of 1.1985 GBP/USD. The mismatch arises because the FX timing difference (unrealized gain/loss on the intercompany balance) was never posted on either set of books. Under ASC 830, intercompany balances denominated in a currency other than the entity's functional currency require remeasurement at period-end rates, with the resulting gain or loss recorded in earnings (not OCI) unless the intercompany balance is of a long-term investment nature.

**What the agent should catch:** The $47K mismatch, the FX rate discrepancy between how the parent and sub recorded the same transactions, and the need for a remeasurement entry to eliminate the difference before consolidation. The agent should also flag that this needs a journal entry on the US Parent's books to remeasure the receivable at the spot rate, with the difference flowing through the income statement (account 7030 — Other Income/Expense).

**Severity:** Medium — the amount is small relative to total assets, but an auditor will test intercompany elimination as a key audit area. The underlying process gap (no FX remeasurement procedure for IC balances) suggests other intercompany balances may have the same problem.

### Issue 2: ASC 606 Violation — NovaCorp Enterprise Agreement

**Location:** Revenue Recognition Schedule — NovaCorp contract

**The Problem:** The NovaCorp contract is being recognized as a single performance obligation ($4,200,000 ratably over 36 months), but the implementation services involve "significant customization" — custom workflow engine, custom API integrations, custom reporting dashboards, and data migration. These are strong indicators that the implementation services are a separate performance obligation that should be evaluated under ASC 606-10-25-21.

**Why it matters:** Under ASC 606-10-25-21, a good or service is distinct if (a) the customer can benefit from it on its own or together with other readily available resources, and (b) the entity's promise to transfer it is separately identifiable from other promises. Here:
- The platform license has standalone value — other customers use it without custom implementation.
- The implementation services involve significant judgment and customization that goes beyond routine setup — custom workflow engine, API integrations with SAP, custom dashboards, and data migration are specialized services.
- The fact that other customers use the platform without this level of customization demonstrates the platform has standalone value.
- The implementation services should be a separate performance obligation, likely recognized over time (Oct 2025–Apr 2026) using an input method (hours or costs incurred).
- Premium support is likely a third performance obligation, recognized ratably over the support period.

**Impact:** The $350,000 recognized in Q4 is likely overstated for the platform license portion (which should begin recognizing at or after go-live, when the customer obtains control) and understated for implementation services (which should be recognized as services are performed). The allocation should be based on standalone selling prices. This is a potential restatement risk if the pattern applies to the other 11 "enterprise platform deals" in the contract portfolio.

**Severity:** High — ASC 606 revenue recognition errors are the #1 cause of SEC restatements. The close memo's rationale uses language that essentially argues the customer can't benefit from the platform without the implementation — but the contract portfolio shows 842 standard SaaS subscriptions that don't require custom implementation, proving the platform has standalone value.

### Issue 3: Missing Lease — ASC 842

**Location:** Lease Schedule vs. Management Discussion Points

**The Problem:** The lease schedule shows 4 leases, all commencing before Q4 2025. But the management discussion notes that CloudMesh ended Q4 with 482 employees (up from 448 in Q3) and expanded headcount significantly in India. The India Dev Center lease (L-003) commenced June 2023 with a 3-year term — it expires June 2026. With 12 new engineers added to the India center, there is no new lease for the expanded space.

More critically, there is no evidence of any new lease signed in Q4 2025 despite significant headcount growth across the organization. A company growing from 380 to 482 employees in one year typically needs additional office space. If a new office lease was signed in Q4 but not yet recorded, this represents an off-balance-sheet liability — exactly the scenario ASC 842 was designed to prevent.

**What the agent should ask:** Was any new lease signed in Q4 2025 (or are any leases under negotiation) for additional office space to accommodate the headcount expansion? The lease schedule appears static since 2024 despite substantial workforce growth.

**Severity:** Medium-High — A missed lease is a balance sheet error (understated ROU assets and lease liabilities). If the lease is material (e.g., $50K/month for 5 years = ~$2.6M present value), it could be a material misstatement. The auditor will test for lease completeness by comparing the lease schedule to real estate records, vendor payments, and new contracts.

**Severity:** High — Material unexplained variance in a catch-all account category is a classic audit red flag. The dismissive narrative ("in line with expectations") when the variance is 285% over budget raises questions about the quality of management's review process.

### Issue 5: Bonus Accrual Based on 100% Plan Achievement Despite 94% ARR Attainment

**Location:** Flux Analysis — Accrued Compensation line + Management Discussion Points

**The Problem:** The flux analysis states the bonus pool of $3,200,000 is "calculated at 100% of plan achievement based on annual revenue target of $128.0M." But the Key Metrics Dashboard shows ARR of $120.4M — which is 94% of the $128.0M target. The company missed its revenue target.

**What the agent should catch:**
- If the bonus plan pays at 100% for 100% target achievement, and the company achieved 94%, the accrual should reflect the actual achievement level — not the full 100% payout.
- Typical bonus plans have a payout curve: 0% below threshold (e.g., 85%), linear between threshold and target, 100% at target, accelerator above target. At 94% attainment, the payout might be 85-94% of target bonus, not 100%.
- Overstating the bonus accrual by $200K-$500K (depending on the actual payout curve) overstates accrued compensation and understates net income.
- Alternatively, if the bonus plan has a different metric (e.g., bookings, not ARR), this should be documented. The close memo should clearly state what metric drives the bonus and the actual achievement level.
- This is a SOX risk — the bonus accrual is a significant estimate that requires documented support and management review.

**Severity:** Medium-High — The dollar impact may be $200K-$500K (estimated), which is meaningful relative to a $4.6M net loss. More importantly, accruing at 100% when the metric shows 94% suggests either (a) the accrual is wrong, (b) the metric is wrong, or (c) the bonus plan uses a different achievement measure that isn't documented in the close package. Any of these is a problem.

### Issue 6: UK Subsidiary P&L Translated at Spot Rate Instead of Average Rate

**Location:** UK Subsidiary Standalone Trial Balance

**The Problem:** The UK Sub income statement is translated into USD using the December 31 spot rate of 1.1990 GBP/USD. Under ASC 830-10-45-3, income statement items of a foreign subsidiary whose functional currency is the local currency (GBP) must be translated at the average exchange rate for the period — not the spot rate.

**The correct approach:**
- Balance sheet items: spot rate (1.1990) — this is correct.
- Income statement items: weighted-average rate for Q4 (1.2633) — this was NOT used.
- The consolidation should translate UK revenue of £3,380,000 at the average rate: £3,380,000 x 1.2633 = $4,269,954. Instead, the consolidation shows £3,380,000 x 1.1990 = $4,052,620.
- **Revenue understatement: $217,334** ($4,269,954 - $4,052,620)
- Similarly, total expenses of £3,120,000 should be $3,941,496 (at average rate) instead of $3,740,880 (at spot rate).
- **Expense understatement: $200,616** ($3,941,496 - $3,740,880)
- **Net income impact: understated by approximately $16,718** (the net of revenue and expense understatement)
- The difference between average rate translation and spot rate translation should flow through Accumulated Other Comprehensive Income (AOCI) as a cumulative translation adjustment (CTA) — not through the income statement.

**What the agent should catch:** Every P&L line in the UK Sub consolidation workpaper uses 1.1990 (spot rate). The Q4 average rate of 1.2633 is disclosed in the supplementary FX rates section but was not used. This is a mechanical translation error that affects every consolidated income statement line item.

**Severity:** High — While the net income impact (~$17K) is small, the gross misstatement of revenue ($217K) and expenses ($201K) is more significant. More critically, using the wrong translation rate is a fundamental ASC 830 error that suggests the consolidation process for the UK Sub is not properly designed. An auditor will test currency translation methodology as a standard procedure — this error would be caught in audit and would raise questions about the competence of the consolidation process.
