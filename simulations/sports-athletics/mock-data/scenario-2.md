**Analysis:** On high-demand nights (Friday/Saturday, fireworks), secondary market prices are 50% above face value — the organization is selling $42 tickets for $28. On low-demand nights (Tuesday/Wednesday), tickets are available for $8 on the secondary market, meaning the organization's $28 face value is a barrier to walk-up sales. A basic dynamic pricing implementation (price floors on low-demand, price ceilings on high-demand) could capture $180K-$240K in incremental revenue annually.

## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **Season ticket renewal rate dropped due to pricing, not product quality:** Renewal rate fell from 78.2% to 61.4% — a 16.8 percentage point drop. The exit survey is unambiguous: 72% of non-renewals cite "ticket price increase too steep" as a reason, and 48% note they can buy cheaper on the secondary market. Meanwhile, current holder satisfaction is 8.2/10 and NPS is +32 — the product is good, the price isn't. The organization raised season ticket prices ~18% in one year. The "value for money" score (5.4/10) is the only satisfaction metric below 7.0. The fix is pricing, not product improvement. A rollback or freeze on pricing for 2026, combined with added-value perks (exclusive events, early entry, food credits), would likely recover a significant portion of lost renewals.

2. **Massive day-of-week attendance gap with no dynamic pricing strategy:** Friday games are at 95% capacity while Tuesday games are at 32.7%. Secondary market data shows Friday field box tickets resell at $42 (50% above $28 face) while Tuesday tickets resell at $8 (71% below face). The organization uses flat pricing — the same $28 field box ticket for a Tuesday game against nobody and a Friday fireworks night. A basic dynamic pricing model (lower prices on weekday games to drive attendance, premium pricing on high-demand nights to capture willingness-to-pay) would generate an estimated $180K-$240K in incremental annual revenue. This is the single highest-impact revenue optimization available.

3. **$340K in unsold sponsorship inventory with 40% of previous sponsors uncontacted:** The sponsorship sales team has $340,800 in unsold inventory, and 17 of 42 previous sponsors (40.5%) haven't even been contacted about 2026 renewals — including 4 who spent $15,000+ in 2025. This is a basic sales execution failure. The one-person sponsorship sales team clearly lacks capacity. The estimated incremental revenue from simply contacting previous sponsors is $94K-$142K. The organization should prioritize (a) immediately contacting the 17 uncontacted sponsors, starting with the 4 high-value accounts, (b) hiring a part-time sales assistant or outsourcing outreach, and (c) creating multi-year deal incentives to lock in renewals and reduce annual churn.

4. **Fan email list is a deliverability disaster:** 45,200 email addresses with a 12.4% open rate (vs. 22-28% industry benchmark), 8.2% bounce rate, and 37.8% of the list (17,110 addresses) inactive for 12+ months. The list has never been cleaned. Zero segmented campaigns have been sent — season ticket holders (who have already bought) receive the same "buy tickets" messages as people who signed up once and never purchased. The fix: (a) immediately remove or suppress the 17K inactive addresses, (b) implement behavioral segmentation (STH, multi-game buyers, single-game, never-purchased), (c) shift content strategy from 80% promotional to value-driven content by segment. Expected lift: open rate from 12% to 20%+, with corresponding improvements in click-through and conversion.

5. **Zero e-commerce presence for merchandise:** 85% of merchandise revenue ($241K of $284K) comes from game-day sales only. The team has no online store. MiLB teams with e-commerce average 28-35% of merch revenue online — the River Cats are missing an estimated $110K-$135K in annual revenue. This also limits the team's brand reach: fans who can't attend games have no way to purchase merchandise. The per-cap merch spend ($0.77 vs. $1.20 MiLB average) also suggests the in-stadium assortment and merchandising execution need improvement. Launching a Shopify or BigCommerce store with the team's top SKUs plus online-exclusive items is a high-ROI project.

6. **Social media follower growth is strong but engagement rate is abysmal:** Total followers grew 18.4% to 46,200, but weighted average engagement rate is just 0.8%. The content analysis reveals why: 60% of posts are the lowest-engagement content types (score updates at 0.4%, ticket promos at 0.3%), while the highest-engagement types (behind-the-scenes at 3.1%, fan content at 2.8%, player features at 2.4%) represent only 13.6% of posts. The team is producing content for itself, not for fans. TikTok — the platform with the highest engagement (2.8%) and fastest growth (+82.4%) — gets the fewest posts (2/week). Social media generated only $17,600 in attributable ticket revenue against ~$35K in costs. The fix: flip the content ratio (60%+ high-engagement content, limit promos to 20%), increase TikTok to 5+ posts/week, and implement a fan-generated content strategy to amplify authenticity.
