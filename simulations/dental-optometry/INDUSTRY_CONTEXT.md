# Industry Context: Dental & Optometry

## What This Industry Does

Dental practices and optometry clinics deliver primary oral and vision healthcare to patients across the lifespan. These practices operate at the intersection of clinical care, insurance administration, retail sales (optical dispensing, cosmetic dentistry), and patient relationship management. The majority are small businesses — solo practitioners or groups of 2-10 providers — though DSO (Dental Service Organization) and OD-affiliated models are rapidly consolidating the landscape.

Dental practices provide preventive care (cleanings, exams, radiographs), restorative dentistry (fillings, crowns, bridges), prosthodontics (dentures, implants), endodontics (root canals), periodontics (gum disease treatment), oral surgery (extractions, wisdom teeth), orthodontics, and cosmetic procedures (veneers, whitening). Revenue is driven by a mix of insurance reimbursement and patient out-of-pocket payments, with case acceptance rate — the percentage of diagnosed treatment that patients actually schedule — being the single most important operational metric most practices fail to optimize.

Optometry practices provide comprehensive eye exams, contact lens fittings, diagnosis and management of ocular disease (glaucoma, macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy), and pre/post-operative care for refractive surgery. Most optometry practices include an optical dispensary selling prescription eyeglasses, sunglasses, and contact lenses — a retail component that can represent 40-60% of practice revenue. The critical operational challenge is insurance complexity: a single patient visit may involve both a vision plan (routine exam, glasses benefit) and medical insurance (diagnosis-driven exam for a medical condition), and billing to the wrong plan leaves thousands of dollars on the table annually.

Key operational functions include: scheduling and patient flow optimization, insurance verification and benefits maximization, treatment planning and case presentation, hygiene department management (dental), optical dispensing and inventory management (optometry), recall and reactivation systems, collections and accounts receivable management, and regulatory compliance (HIPAA, OSHA, state practice acts).

## Key Tools & Platforms

### Dental Practice Management Systems
- **Dentrix (Henry Schein)** — The dominant dental practice management system, used by approximately 35% of US dental practices. Handles scheduling, charting, treatment planning, insurance claims processing, patient communication, and financial reporting. The Dentrix ecosystem includes Dentrix Ascend (cloud-based), Dentrix Enterprise (DSOs), and integrations with digital imaging (Dexis), patient communication (Dentrix Communication Manager), and analytics.
- **Eaglesoft (Patterson Dental)** — The second-largest dental PMS. Strong in treatment planning visualization, insurance management, and clinical charting. Often paired with Patterson's imaging systems and supply chain.
- **Open Dental** — Open-source dental practice management software with a passionate user community. Lower cost than Dentrix/Eaglesoft, highly customizable, strong reporting capabilities. Growing rapidly among tech-savvy practices and DSOs who want data control.
- **Curve Dental** — Cloud-native dental PMS. Popular with newer practices that want to avoid server infrastructure. Strong patient portal and online scheduling.
- **Dental Intelligence** — Analytics and performance dashboard that sits on top of existing PMS. Provides KPI tracking (production, collections, case acceptance, hygiene metrics, schedule optimization), morning huddle reports, and automated patient outreach. The industry's leading business intelligence tool.
- **OrthoFi** — Patient financing and practice management platform specifically for orthodontic practices. Handles treatment presentations, flexible payment plans, and insurance billing for orthodontics.

### Optometry Practice Management & EHR
- **Crystal PM** — Practice management system designed specifically for optometry. Handles scheduling, billing, inventory management, and reporting. Known for optical dispensary management and frame inventory tracking.
- **RevolutionEHR** — Cloud-based EHR and practice management for optometry. Strong in clinical documentation, medical billing, and optical point-of-sale. Integrates with major vision plans (VSP, EyeMed) for real-time eligibility verification.
- **Officemate/EyefinityEHR (VSP)** — Practice management and EHR owned by VSP Global. Deep integration with VSP vision plan for claims and authorization. Common among VSP-affiliated practices.
- **Compulink Advantage** — EHR and practice management for ophthalmology and optometry. Strong in medical billing, surgical scheduling, and multi-location management.

### Patient Communication & Engagement
- **Weave** — All-in-one patient communication platform covering phone systems, texting, email, reviews, payments, and scheduling. Extremely popular in dental and optometry. Provides missed call text-back, automated appointment reminders, and review generation.
- **Lighthouse 360** — Automated patient communication for dental practices. Handles appointment confirmations, recall reminders, reactivation campaigns, and online reputation management.
- **RevenueWell** — Patient engagement platform for dental practices. Marketing automation, patient communications, online scheduling, and digital forms. Focuses on filling the schedule and reducing no-shows.
- **Solutionreach** — Patient relationship management for healthcare practices. Automated recalls, reminders, surveys, and group messaging campaigns.
- **Modento** — Digital forms, patient communication, and online scheduling for dental practices. Eliminates paper intake forms.

### Patient Financing
- **CareCredit (Synchrony)** — The dominant patient financing platform in dental and optometry. Offers patients promotional financing (0% APR for 6-24 months) for treatment not fully covered by insurance. Practices pay a merchant discount fee (typically 5-14% depending on promotional period).
- **Sunbit** — Point-of-sale lending platform for dental and optical. Approves ~85% of applicants with a soft credit check. Gaining market share from CareCredit due to higher approval rates and simpler patient experience.
- **LendingClub Patient Solutions** — Patient financing with fixed monthly payments. Used for larger treatment plans (implants, full-mouth rehabilitation, orthodontics).
- **Cherry** — Buy-now-pay-later platform for dental and medical aesthetics. Patient-friendly split payment options.

### Insurance & Billing
- **VSP Provider Portal** — Portal for VSP Vision Care network providers. Eligibility verification, prior authorization for contact lenses, claims submission, and payment tracking.
- **EyeMed Provider Portal** — Portal for EyeMed Vision Care network. Similar functionality to VSP portal. EyeMed is the second-largest vision plan, owned by Luxottica/EssilorLuxottica.
- **DentalXChange** — Electronic claims clearinghouse for dental insurance. Handles claim submission, ERA (electronic remittance advice) processing, and real-time eligibility verification across multiple payers.
- **Vyne Dental (formerly NEA)** — Dental claims management including electronic attachments (radiographs, clinical notes) required by many payers for claim adjudication.
- **Trojan Professional Services** — Insurance plan analysis and fee schedule optimization for dental practices. Analyzes PPO fee schedules and recommends fee adjustments to maximize reimbursement.

## Key Frameworks & Methodologies

### ADA CDT Coding (Current Dental Terminology)
The American Dental Association's CDT code set is the HIPAA-mandated standard code set for dental procedures. Updated annually with additions, revisions, and deletions. Dental claims must use CDT codes (D-codes), not CPT codes, with the exception of certain oral surgery procedures that cross over to medical billing. Understanding CDT coding is essential for proper claim submission, and incorrect coding is the primary cause of claim denials.

### Medical Billing in Optometry (CPT/HCPCS)
Optometry practices must determine whether each patient encounter is a "routine vision exam" (billed to vision plan using V-codes/S-codes) or a "medical exam" (billed to medical insurance using CPT evaluation and management codes plus ICD-10 diagnosis codes). The distinction hinges on whether the primary purpose of the visit is refractive/routine or driven by a medical diagnosis (diabetes, glaucoma, macular degeneration, dry eye). Practices that default to vision plan billing when medical billing is warranted leave significant revenue on the table — medical reimbursement rates are typically 2-4x higher than vision plan allowances.

### Case Acceptance Rate Optimization
The percentage of diagnosed treatment value that patients schedule and complete. Industry benchmark is 85% for comprehensive treatment plans. Most practices operate at 40-60%. Optimization involves: verbal skills training for treatment coordinators, visual aids and intraoral camera use during case presentation, same-day treatment scheduling, financial options presentation (insurance + financing + payment plans), and systematic follow-up on unscheduled treatment.

### Treatment Plan Presentation
Structured approach to presenting diagnosed treatment to patients. Best practice frameworks include: (1) clinical findings presented in patient-friendly language with visual aids, (2) consequences of non-treatment, (3) treatment options from ideal to acceptable, (4) financial breakdown showing insurance coverage + patient portion + financing options, (5) scheduling while the patient is still in the office. The financial conversation should never happen in the clinical operatory — it moves to the treatment coordinator's space.

### Insurance Benefits Maximization
Strategic approach to scheduling and billing that maximizes insurance benefit utilization for patients. Includes: splitting treatment across benefit years when annual maximums are involved, proper sequencing of procedures to stay within frequency limitations, correct plan-of-benefits interpretation for covered services, pre-authorization for high-value procedures, and coordination of benefits when patients have dual coverage.

### Optical Dispensing Metrics
Key metrics for optometry optical departments: capture rate (percentage of exam patients who purchase eyewear from the practice vs. going elsewhere), average sale per dispensing, frame-to-lens ratio, units per dispensing (multiple pair sales), contact lens annual supply rate, frame inventory turnover, and gross margin by product category. Benchmark capture rate is 65%, and every percentage point below that represents direct revenue leakage.

### Recall & Reactivation Workflows
Systematic processes for bringing patients back for routine care. Recall = patients due for their next scheduled visit (typically 6 months for dental hygiene, 12 months for eye exams). Reactivation = patients who are overdue and have fallen out of the recall cycle (typically 18+ months since last visit). Effective systems use multi-channel outreach (text, email, phone, direct mail) on automated schedules with escalating urgency.

### Hygiene Department Optimization (Dental)
The hygiene department should produce 30-33% of total practice production. Key metrics: hygienist production per hour, perio diagnosis rate (percentage of patients with periodontal disease actually diagnosed and treated vs. maintained in prophylaxis), hygiene reappointment rate, and same-day treatment acceptance for restorative findings diagnosed during hygiene visits. Underperforming hygiene departments almost always have a perio underdiagnosis problem.

## Regulations

### HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act)
Applies to all dental and optometry practices as covered entities. Privacy Rule governs use and disclosure of Protected Health Information (PHI). Security Rule mandates administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for electronic PHI (ePHI). Breach Notification Rule requires notification to affected individuals, HHS, and (for large breaches) media. Common violations in dental/optometry: patient charts visible at front desk, unencrypted emails containing PHI, shared login credentials for PMS, inadequate Business Associate Agreements with IT vendors and cloud services.

### State Dental Practice Acts
Each state regulates dentistry through its dental board. Practice acts define scope of practice for dentists, dental hygienists, dental assistants, and expanded function dental assistants (EFDAs). Key variations include: which procedures dental hygienists can perform under general vs. direct supervision, whether hygienists can administer local anesthesia, EFDA scope of practice, and continuing education requirements. Practice act violations can result in license suspension or revocation.

### State Optometry Practice Acts
State optometry boards regulate scope of practice for optometrists (ODs). Scope varies significantly by state — some states allow ODs to perform certain laser procedures and minor surgical procedures, others restrict to diagnosis and pharmaceutical management. All states allow diagnostic pharmaceutical agents (dilation drops) and therapeutic pharmaceutical agents (antibiotics, anti-inflammatories, glaucoma medications). Practice act defines supervision requirements for opticians and optical technicians.

### OSHA Compliance
Dental practices must comply with OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030) — exposure control plan, annual training, hepatitis B vaccination for at-risk employees, sharps injury log. Hazard Communication Standard (GHS-aligned) — Safety Data Sheets for all chemicals, labeling, employee training. Dental-specific concerns include nitrous oxide exposure monitoring, amalgam handling, and sterilization protocols. Optometry practices have lower OSHA exposure but must comply with hazard communication for lens treatments and laboratory chemicals.

### ADA Standards
American Dental Association standards for infection control, radiography, and clinical practice. ADA Standards Committee on Dental Products evaluates dental materials, instruments, and equipment. ADA Seal of Acceptance program for dental products. While ADA standards are not legally binding (unlike OSHA regulations), they represent the standard of care and are referenced in malpractice litigation.

### FDA Regulations
- **Dental Devices:** FDA regulates dental materials (composites, amalgam, cements), dental implants, dental lasers, digital radiography equipment, CAD/CAM systems, and orthodontic appliances. Most dental devices are Class II (510(k) clearance required).
- **Contact Lenses:** FDA classifies contact lenses as medical devices. Daily wear soft lenses are Class II; extended wear lenses and rigid gas permeable lenses are Class III (PMA required for new designs). All contact lens prescriptions must include brand name, lens parameters, and prescriber information.
- **FTC Contact Lens Rule (Fairness to Contact Lens Consumers Act, 2003):** Requires prescribers to provide patients with a copy of their contact lens prescription immediately after fitting, without requiring the patient to ask. Prescribers must verify prescription requests from third-party sellers within 8 business hours. Violations carry FTC enforcement action and fines.

### State Board CE Requirements
All states require continuing education for license renewal. Dental CE requirements typically range from 20-40 hours per renewal cycle (1-2 years). Optometry CE requirements typically range from 20-36 hours per renewal cycle. Many states mandate specific CE topics: infection control, opioid prescribing, jurisprudence, CPR/BLS, and cultural competency. COPE (Council on Optometric Practitioner Education) approves CE for optometrists.

## Common Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **CDT Code** | Current Dental Terminology code — the HIPAA-mandated code set for dental procedures, maintained by the ADA (e.g., D0120, D1110, D2740) |
| **D-Code** | Shorthand for CDT procedure code. D0xxx = diagnostics, D1xxx = preventive, D2xxx = restorative, D4xxx = periodontics, D5xxx = prosthodontics, D6xxx = implants, D7xxx = oral surgery, D8xxx = orthodontics |
| **UCR Fee** | Usual, Customary, and Reasonable fee — the fee a dentist charges for a procedure before any insurance adjustment. Also called the "full fee" or "office fee" |
| **Allowable Amount** | The maximum amount an insurance plan will pay for a covered procedure. The difference between the UCR fee and allowable amount is a write-off for in-network providers |
| **Deductible** | The dollar amount a patient must pay out-of-pocket before dental/vision insurance begins paying. Typically $50-$100 for individual, $150-$300 for family in dental plans |
| **Annual Maximum** | The maximum dollar amount a dental insurance plan will pay per covered individual per benefit year. Typically $1,000-$2,500 for PPO plans. Has barely increased since the 1970s despite inflation |
| **Waiting Period** | The time a patient must be enrolled in a dental plan before certain benefits become available. Common: 6 months for basic procedures, 12 months for major procedures |
| **Frequency Limitation** | Insurance restriction on how often a covered procedure can be performed. Common: prophylaxis 2x per benefit year, BWX 1x per benefit year, crown replacement once per 5 years |
| **Downcoding** | When an insurance company pays for a less expensive procedure than the one submitted. Example: provider submits D2740 (porcelain crown) and insurer pays at D2751 (metal crown) rate. A common and often unjustified payer tactic |
| **Bundling** | When an insurance company denies a separately billed procedure because they consider it included in another procedure already billed. Example: denying a core buildup (D2950) when billed with a crown (D2740) |
| **Pre-authorization/Pre-determination** | Submitting a treatment plan to the insurance company before performing the procedure to get a written estimate of benefits. Not a guarantee of payment but reduces surprise denials |
| **EOB (Explanation of Benefits)** | Document from the insurance company showing how a claim was processed — allowable amount, deductible applied, patient responsibility, and payment amount |
| **ERA (Electronic Remittance Advice)** | Electronic version of the EOB used for automated payment posting in practice management software |
| **Coordination of Benefits (COB)** | Rules for determining which plan pays first (primary) and which pays second (secondary) when a patient has dual insurance coverage |
| **Assignment of Benefits** | Patient authorization allowing insurance payment to go directly to the provider rather than to the patient |
| **Write-off** | The difference between the provider's UCR fee and the insurance allowable amount that an in-network provider cannot collect from the patient |
| **Production** | The total value of services rendered by a provider or practice in a given period, measured at UCR fees. The gross revenue equivalent |
| **Collections** | Actual money received by the practice. Collections rate = collections / production. Benchmark: 98%+ |
| **Adjusted Production** | Production minus contractual write-offs. Represents the maximum collectable revenue. A more meaningful metric than gross production for in-network practices |
| **Case Acceptance Rate** | Percentage of diagnosed treatment value that patients actually schedule and complete. Benchmark: 85% for comprehensive plans |
| **Perio (Periodontal Disease)** | Gum disease ranging from gingivitis (reversible) to periodontitis (irreversible bone loss). Diagnosed by probing depths, bleeding on probing, clinical attachment loss, and radiographic bone levels |
| **Prophylaxis (Prophy)** | Routine dental cleaning for patients with healthy gums. CDT code D1110 (adult) or D1120 (child). Insurance typically covers 2 per year |
| **SRP (Scaling and Root Planing)** | Deep cleaning procedure for periodontal disease. CDT codes D4341/D4342 per quadrant. Higher reimbursement than prophy and clinically necessary when perio is present |
| **Capture Rate (Optical)** | Percentage of exam patients who purchase eyewear from the practice's optical dispensary rather than going to an outside retailer. Benchmark: 65% |
| **Annual Supply (Contact Lens)** | Selling a full year's supply of contact lenses at the time of fitting or exam. Higher margin than partial fills, locks the patient into the practice, and often qualifies for manufacturer rebates |
| **AR Coating** | Anti-reflective coating applied to lenses. A high-margin add-on that most patients benefit from. Key driver of average sale per dispensing |
| **Frame Board** | The physical display of frames in an optical dispensary. Managed by inventory turns, brand mix, price point distribution, and demographic fit |
| **PD (Pupillary Distance)** | The distance between the centers of the pupils, measured in millimeters. Required for accurately positioning lenses in frames. Online retailers need this measurement, so some practices are reluctant to include it on prescriptions (though some states require it) |
| **Recall** | The system for scheduling patients for their next routine visit (6-month hygiene, annual eye exam). Effective recall is the engine of practice sustainability |
| **Reactivation** | Outreach to patients who have fallen out of the recall cycle (typically 18+ months overdue). These are patients the practice is actively losing |
| **Hygiene Reappointment Rate** | Percentage of hygiene patients who schedule their next hygiene appointment before leaving the office. Benchmark: 90%+ |
| **Chair Time** | The time a patient occupies an operatory/exam chair. Efficient chair time utilization is the primary constraint on practice production |
| **Morning Huddle** | Daily pre-shift meeting (10-15 minutes) where the team reviews the day's schedule, identifies production opportunities, discusses patient needs, and sets daily goals |

## Common Mistakes in This Field

1. **Defaulting to vision plan billing when medical billing is warranted.** When an optometry patient presents with a medical diagnosis (diabetes, glaucoma, dry eye, macular degeneration), the exam should be billed to medical insurance using CPT E/M codes and ICD-10 diagnosis codes — not to the vision plan. Medical reimbursement is typically $150-$300+ vs. $40-$80 from vision plans. Practices that don't distinguish between medical and routine exams leave tens of thousands of dollars annually in uncollected revenue.

2. **Underdiagnosing periodontal disease in hygiene.** Many dental hygienists continue performing prophylaxis (D1110) on patients who clinically present with periodontal disease (probing depths >4mm, bleeding on probing, radiographic bone loss) because "the patient has always gotten a regular cleaning." This is both a clinical and financial failure — SRP (D4341/D4342) is the appropriate treatment for active periodontal disease, reimburses at a higher rate, and is the standard of care. Performing a prophy on a perio patient is undertreating the disease.

3. **Not verifying insurance benefits before the patient's visit.** When insurance eligibility and benefits aren't verified until the patient is in the chair (or worse, after treatment), the practice risks: performing services not covered by the plan, misquoting patient out-of-pocket costs, performing procedures subject to waiting periods or frequency limitations, and patient dissatisfaction when the bill is higher than expected. Pre-visit verification should be standard for every scheduled patient.

4. **Presenting treatment cost before clinical value.** When the first thing a patient hears after diagnosis is the price tag, case acceptance drops dramatically. Effective case presentation follows a sequence: clinical findings (what's happening), consequences (what happens if untreated), treatment options (what we can do), and only then financials (what it costs and how we make it affordable). Leading with cost turns a healthcare decision into a purchasing decision.

5. **Ignoring optical capture rate and contact lens annual supply metrics.** Optometry practices that don't actively track and manage optical capture rate (benchmark: 65%) and annual supply rate (benchmark: 50%+) are hemorrhaging revenue. Every patient who walks out without buying glasses or a year's worth of contacts is a patient buying from Warby Parker, Zenni, 1-800 Contacts, or Costco. The exam chair generates the prescription — the optical should capture the revenue.

## Excellent vs. Dangerous Work

### Excellent Work Looks Like
- Insurance verification completed 48 hours before every appointment with benefits breakdown documented in the patient chart
- Treatment plans presented with intraoral photos, clear explanations of clinical findings, and financial options including insurance coverage, patient portion, and third-party financing
- Hygiene department running comprehensive periodontal assessments with full-mouth probing at every hygiene visit, correctly diagnosing and coding perio when present
- Collections rate at 98%+ with insurance aging over 90 days under 5% of total AR
- Optical dispensary tracking capture rate, annual supply rate, and average sale weekly, with targeted strategies to improve each
- Medical vs. vision billing decision made correctly on every optometry encounter based on the presenting diagnosis
- Recall system achieving 90%+ reappointment rate with automated multi-channel outreach for overdue patients
- Morning huddles that review the day's schedule for production opportunities, unscheduled treatment, and insurance benefit utilization

### Dangerous Work Looks Like
- Billing all optometry visits to vision plans regardless of presenting diagnosis
- Performing prophylaxis on patients with active periodontal disease
- No insurance verification process — patients learn their costs after treatment
- Treatment plans presented as a number on a piece of paper with no clinical context or financial options
- Recall limited to a single postcard with no follow-up for patients who don't respond
- Collections rate below 90% with no one assigned to work insurance aging reports
- Frame inventory that hasn't been audited in over a year with dead stock taking up board space
- No HIPAA training, no OSHA compliance program, no written policies for either
