## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **Three uninsurable drivers in standard markets:** The broker's MVR summary categorizes all drivers as "clean" or "minor violations." However, the raw MVR appendix data reveals three drivers who would be unacceptable in most standard commercial auto programs:
   - **D-004 (Tyler Branch):** Three violations in 36 months — HOS violation, logbook violation, and following too closely. Pattern of regulatory non-compliance.
   - **D-008 (Marcus Johnson):** DUI arrest reduced to reckless driving plus a subsequent speeding violation. Even a reduced DUI is a decline in most commercial auto underwriting guidelines.
   - **D-017 (Michael Petrov):** Three violations including overweight, unsafe lane change, and a CDL that was expired for 30 days (meaning he was driving without a valid CDL during that period). CDL lapse is a serious regulatory violation.
   These drivers are buried in the appendix data and not flagged in the broker's summary, which describes only "minor violations" and "preventable accidents."

2. **Loss ratio trend misrepresented:** The broker's narrative describes the loss experience as "stable" and attributes the increase in claims to fleet growth. The actual loss ratios tell a different story: 45% → 52% → 58% → 67% → 74%. This is a clear upward trend. Even on a per-unit basis, the loss ratio is deteriorating (more losses per truck per year). The current-year 74% loss ratio on commercial auto — before allocated loss adjustment expenses — would produce a combined ratio well over 100%. Additionally, the non-renewal by Great Plains Mutual contradicts the broker's claim that the carrier is "exiting the segment." Carriers non-renew unprofitable accounts.

3. **Hazardous materials requiring MCS-90 and pollution liability:** The operations footnote reveals that Mountain West hauls cleaning chemicals and industrial solvents (acetone, MEK, toluene) approximately 30-40 times per year. These are regulated hazardous materials under 49 CFR 172.101, exceeding materials of trade exceptions. This triggers mandatory MCS-90 endorsement requirements and necessitates pollution liability coverage — neither of which appear in the coverage request. Only 4 of 28 drivers hold hazmat endorsements, meaning the other 24 cannot legally transport these materials. If an unendorsed driver hauls hazmat and has an accident, the coverage implications are severe.

4. **Overinsured older trucks — potential moral hazard:** Units #138 (2011 Freightliner Columbia) and #139 (2010 Kenworth T660) are 15-16 years old. The fleet schedule lists both at $85,000 physical damage value. Actual market value for trucks of this age and type is $10,000-$15,000. Insuring them at $85,000 creates a significant overinsurance situation where a total loss claim would pay 5-8x the actual value of the truck. This is a classic moral hazard indicator. At minimum, the stated values need to be adjusted to actual cash value; at maximum, this warrants questions about the applicant's intent.

5. **Rapid fleet growth without safety infrastructure:** Mountain West grew from 33 to 45 trucks in one year (36% growth) but has no dedicated safety director. Safety oversight is handled by the Operations Manager who is also responsible for dispatch. DOT requires motor carriers with 20+ power units to demonstrate adequate safety management systems, and the increase in claim frequency correlates directly with the growth period. The company has crossed the threshold where a part-time safety function is inadequate — peer companies of this size typically employ a full-time safety director. The absence of formal safety infrastructure combined with deteriorating loss ratios and problem drivers suggests systemic safety culture issues.
