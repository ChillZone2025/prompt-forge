## PLANTED ISSUES (for evaluation — agent should catch these)

1. **Unreasonable delay in opening investigation:** The complaint was filed on February 10, 2026, and the investigation was not opened until March 3 — a 21-day delay. Best practice (and what most courts consider "prompt") is to begin an investigation within 1-3 business days of receiving a complaint. A 3-week delay exposes the company to liability for failure to take prompt remedial action under Title VII. During those 21 days, Maya continued to work in proximity to Viktor with no protective measures. If Viktor engaged in additional harassing behavior during that period, NovaTech bears heightened liability for failing to act.

2. **Witness interviewed with respondent's manager present:** Derek Rawlings (Witness 3) was interviewed with Craig Donovan present. Donovan is the Operations Director who approved Viktor's consulting engagement and manages the Apex Talent Group relationship — he has a direct interest in the investigation outcome. His presence during a witness interview creates an inherent intimidation dynamic, whether intentional or not. Derek's testimony was notably more favorable to Viktor than the other witnesses' accounts. Additionally, Donovan was present during Viktor's own interview, which is less problematic (respondent can choose a support person) but combined with his presence at a witness interview, creates an appearance of partiality. The investigator should have required all witness interviews to be conducted without management presence.

3. **Prior complaint not escalated — pattern of non-response:** Maya raised concerns about Viktor to her then-manager Tom Bradley in June 2025. Bradley's documented response — "he's just like that and to let me know if it continues" — demonstrates that NovaTech was on notice of Viktor's behavior 8 months before the formal complaint. Bradley did not escalate to HR, did not document a formal complaint, and dismissed Maya's concern with a minimizing characterization. This prior notice is legally significant: under Title VII, employer liability for harassment by non-supervisors exists when the employer knew or should have known about the harassment and failed to take prompt corrective action. NovaTech knew (through its agent, Bradley) and took no action. Bradley has since left the company, but his knowledge is imputed to the organization.

4. **Anti-harassment policy doesn't cover contractor-to-employee harassment:** Section 7.3.1 (Scope) explicitly states the policy applies to "all NovaTech employees, including full-time, part-time, temporary employees, and interns." It does not mention contractors, consultants, vendors, or third-party workers. Viktor Andersen is a contractor through Apex Talent Group — he is not a NovaTech employee. This is a critical policy gap. While NovaTech still has a legal obligation under Title VII to provide a harassment-free workplace regardless of the harasser's employment status, the absence of contractors from the policy scope creates an enforcement gap: what corrective action authority does NovaTech have over an Apex Talent Group contractor? The policy should be updated to explicitly cover all individuals in the workplace, including contractors, vendors, and visitors. Additionally, the Apex Talent Group master services agreement should be reviewed for provisions regarding contractor conduct and removal.

5. **Interviewer bias in documentation:** The complainant's interview notes are notably less detailed and more clinically summarized than the respondent's notes. Maya's 45-minute interview produced a summary of roughly 200 words. Viktor's 65-minute interview produced a much more detailed account with extensive direct quotes and contextual framing. Viktor's alternative explanations are presented in detail ("outgoing, European personality," "normal compliment," "honest oversight"), while Maya's emotional state is noted but her specific language and detailed account are summarized rather than captured. The credibility notes for Viktor include substantive analysis of contradictions, while Maya's credibility assessment reads as a checklist. A well-conducted investigation should document all interviews with equivalent rigor and detail. The asymmetry in documentation suggests (whether intentionally or not) that the investigator gave more weight and attention to the respondent's version of events.

6. **No interim protective measures implemented:** From the date the complaint was filed (February 10) through the current investigation status (mid-March), no interim protective measures have been implemented. Maya continues to work in the same open workspace adjacent to Viktor on the days he is on-site. She has self-protectively started working from home on Wednesdays, but this effectively punishes the complainant by forcing her to modify her own work arrangements. Best practice — and what courts expect — is that employers implement interim measures to protect the complainant during the investigation period. Appropriate measures could include: temporarily reassigning Viktor to a different workspace or team, adjusting Viktor's on-site schedule to minimize overlap with Maya, issuing a no-contact directive, or (in serious cases) placing the respondent on administrative leave pending investigation. The failure to implement any protective measures during a 5+ week investigation period is a significant procedural failure that amplifies the company's liability.
